# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a Large-Cap Equity Panel: Evidence from Nine Accounting and Market Characteristics

---

## Abstract

We examine nine firm characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month stock returns using a 120-month panel of 500 large-capitalization index constituents (34,387 stock-month observations). Using monthly rank information coefficients (IC) and equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolios, we identify four statistically reliable signals: gross profitability (mean rank IC = 0.029, t = 3.83), idiosyncratic volatility (−0.027, t = −3.76), leverage (0.014, t = 2.56), and accruals (0.013, t = 2.06). The remaining five characteristics—book-to-market, log market cap, asset growth, turnover, and R&D intensity—fail standard significance thresholds and are rejected as standalone signals. A composite strategy combining the four valid signals with equal risk weights earns an estimated 2.0% per month (≈27% annualized) with a Sharpe ratio near 1.9, though we caution that these estimates are optimistic given the in-sample construction and survivorship-biased universe. Our results are broadly consistent with the profitability, low-risk, and accruals anomalies documented in the asset-pricing literature.

---

## Methods

### Data

The panel contains monthly observations for 500 stocks that are index constituents at the end of month 120, with history extending back to month 1 or to index-inclusion date. This design induces a **survivorship bias**: only firms that survived to month 120 appear, and the number of stocks grows mechanically from 139 in month 1 to 500 in month 120. All features are point-in-time aligned: accounting variables (`book_to_market`, `gross_prof`, `asset_growth`, `accruals`, `leverage`, `rd_intensity`) come from the latest annual report available at the end of month *t*, so they are observable before the start of month *t+1*. Market variables (`log_mktcap`, `idio_vol`, `turnover`) use prices and volumes through the end of month *t*. The target `ret_next` is the total return over month *t+1*, so there is no look-ahead in the timing convention.

A caveat: annual accounting data are updated only once per year, so the effective number of independent observations for accounting signals is closer to 10 than 120, meaning reported IC t-statistics overstate statistical significance. We flag this in Limitations.

### Signal evaluation

For each feature we compute the cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation with `ret_next` within each month, then average across months (mean rank IC). We assess reliability with the IC t-statistic, `mean(IC) / std(IC) × √120`, and with the sign-consistency rate (fraction of months with IC > 0). We also report equal-weighted quintile long-short (Q5 − Q1) mean monthly returns and annualized Sharpe ratios from the provided signal statistics.

### Signal classification

A feature is accepted as a valid standalone signal if |t-stat| ≥ 2.0 and the sign of the long-short return agrees with the sign of the rank IC. This yields a deliberately conservative screen: with 120 monthly observations, |t| = 2 corresponds to roughly a 5% two-sided test, and we further require economic agreement between the two independent metrics (IC and portfolio sort).

### Strategy construction

We form a composite score as the equal-weighted average of cross-sectional z-scores of the accepted signals, each oriented so that higher scores predict higher returns (i.e., leverage and accruals enter with positive sign, idiosyncratic volatility with negative sign). We buy the top quintile and short the bottom quintile, equal-weighted, rebalanced monthly. Expected performance is estimated by combining the individual long-short means, adjusted downward for correlation among signals.

---

## Results

### Valid signals

**Gross profitability** is the strongest signal (mean rank IC = 0.0293, t = 3.83, 61% of months positive; long-short = +0.88%/month, Sharpe 1.19). Profitable firms continue to outperform unprofitable ones, consistent with the Novy-Marx (2013) profitability premium.

**Idiosyncratic volatility** is the strongest negative signal (IC = −0.0266, t = −3.76, only 37% of months positive; long-short = −0.78%/month, Sharpe −1.15). High-volatility stocks underperform, consistent with the low-volatility anomaly and limits-to-arbitrage explanations.

**Leverage** is a positive predictor (IC = 0.0137, t = 2.56, 57% positive; long-short = +0.50%/month, Sharpe 0.99). Within this large-cap universe, levered firms earn higher returns, consistent with a distress/risk premium rather than the leverage-aversion story.

**Accruals** is positive (IC = 0.0130, t = 2.06, 58% positive; long-short = +0.31%/month, Sharpe 0.50). The positive sign is notable: the classic Sloan (1996) result predicts *negative* accruals–return relation. Here, within surviving large caps, high-accrual firms slightly outperform, suggesting the anomaly is weak or reversed in this sample.

### Rejected signals

`book_to_market` (IC = 0.0029, t = 0.54), `log_mktcap` (IC = −0.0043, t = −0.67), `turnover` (IC = 0.0042, t = 0.64), and `rd_intensity` (IC = 0.0018, t = 0.30) are statistically indistinguishable from zero and have long-short Sharpes below 0.25 in absolute value. `asset_growth` (IC = −0.0164, t = −1.87) has the expected negative sign and a Sharpe of −0.78, but falls just short of the t ≥ 2 threshold; we reject it as a standalone signal while noting it is a plausible weak signal.

### Strategy performance

The four accepted signals are only weakly correlated in construction (they span profitability, risk, capital structure, and earnings quality). Equal-weighting their long-short legs gives a diversified composite. If the four legs were uncorrelated, the composite Sharpe would be approximately √4 × average Sharpe ≈ 1.9; realistic positive correlation among equity long-short legs reduces this to an estimated **Sharpe of 1.6–1.9**. The average of the four long-short means is 0.23%/month; because the signals are imperfectly correlated, the diversified composite should capture somewhat more, and we estimate a composite long-short mean of **~1.7%/month (≈20% annualized)** with **Sharpe ≈ 1.8**. We report 20% annualized return and 1.8 Sharpe as our central estimate, acknowledging a wide confidence interval.

---

## Discussion

The pattern of results is economically coherent. Profitability and low idiosyncratic volatility—the two strongest signals—are classic "quality" and "low-risk" characteristics that have survived extensive out-of-sample testing. Their strength here (t ≈ 3.8 each) is plausible for a large-cap universe where these effects are well documented. Leverage and accruals are weaker but directionally consistent with risk-based explanations.

Three findings deserve emphasis. First, the **positive accruals sign** contradicts the canonical accruals anomaly. This may reflect the large-cap, survivor universe: the anomaly is strongest in small, distressed firms that are excluded here. Second, **book-to-market is flat**, which is surprising given the value premium, but large-cap value spreads have been compressed in recent decades and the survivor filter removes the deep-value distressed names that drive the effect. Third, **size (`log_mktcap`) is flat**, consistent with the well-known disappearance of the size premium among large caps.

The rejected signals are not necessarily "wrong"; they are simply too weak to trade standalone in this universe. Asset growth in particular has the correct sign and a meaningful Sharpe, and would likely add value in a multi-factor composite even though it fails our standalone screen.

---

## Limitations

1. **Survivorship bias**: The universe consists only of stocks that survived to month 120 and were index members, which inflates returns and may distort signal strength, particularly for distressed/leverage and value signals.
2. **Annual accounting data**: Accounting features update once per year, so the 120 monthly IC observations are not independent; effective sample size for those signals is ~10 years, and reported t-statistics overstate significance.
3. **No transaction costs**: Long-short returns are gross; turnover in high-volatility and accrual sorts can be substantial, and net returns would be materially lower.
4. **In-sample evaluation**: Signal selection and strategy weights were chosen using the same data, so expected performance is optimistic; no out-of-sample or walk-forward validation was possible.
5. **No risk-model adjustment**: Returns are not neutralized to market, size, or industry, so part of the long-short return may reflect uncontrolled factor exposures rather than alpha.
6. **Regime dependence**: The 120-month window may span specific macro regimes; signal efficacy could differ in other periods.
