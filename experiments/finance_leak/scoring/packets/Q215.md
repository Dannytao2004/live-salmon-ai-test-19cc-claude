# Predictive Signals in Monthly Stock Returns: Evidence from a Broad Equity Index Panel

## Abstract

This study examines ten firm characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month stock returns using a 120-month panel of 500 stocks per month. We find that analyst forecast revisions, gross profitability, asset growth, and idiosyncratic volatility are statistically significant return predictors. Analyst revisions exhibit the strongest effect, with a monthly rank IC of 0.296 (t=80.29) and a long-short quintile spread of 8.73% per month. Gross profitability positively predicts returns, while asset growth and idiosyncratic volatility negatively predict returns. A composite strategy based on these four signals is expected to deliver attractive risk-adjusted performance.

## Methods

We analyze a monthly panel of 60,000 stock-month observations covering 120 months and 1,217 distinct stocks. Each month, the cross-section contains 500 stocks that were constituents of a broad equity index at that time. The dataset includes ten firm characteristics and the target variable, `ret_next`, defined as the total stock return over the following month.

Our analysis proceeds in three steps. First, we evaluate each feature’s predictive power for `ret_next` using rank information coefficients (Spearman correlation within each month, averaged across months), IC t-statistics, and the consistency of the sign across months. Second, we estimate the economic magnitude of each signal using equal-weighted long-short portfolios that buy the top quintile and sell the bottom quintile of each feature. Third, we identify valid signals based on statistical significance (|t| > 2) and economic materiality, and we construct a simple composite strategy.

We define a valid signal as one with a mean rank IC that is statistically significant at the 5% level and a long-short portfolio with a meaningful monthly return. Features failing these criteria are rejected.

Four features meet our validity criteria:

- **Analyst forecast revisions (`analyst_rev`)** is the strongest signal. Its mean rank IC of 0.2963 is extraordinarily high, with a t-statistic of 80.29 and a positive IC in every one of the 120 months. The long-short quintile portfolio earns 8.73% per month with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 24.23. The direction is positive: stocks with upward analyst revisions outperform those with downward revisions.

- **Gross profitability (`gross_prof`)** is a positive signal. Its mean rank IC is 0.0378 (t=6.07), with 70% of months showing positive IC. The long-short portfolio earns 1.16% per month with an annualized Sharpe of 1.94. More profitable firms, measured by gross profit to total assets, earn higher subsequent returns.

- **Asset growth (`asset_growth`)** is a negative signal. Its mean rank IC is -0.0303 (t=-4.29), with only 39% of months showing positive IC. The long-short portfolio (top minus bottom quintile) loses 0.99% per month, implying that buying low asset-growth stocks and selling high asset-growth stocks would earn 0.99% per month. The annualized Sharpe of the top-minus-bottom portfolio is -1.48, so the reverse (bottom-minus-top) would have a Sharpe of 1.48.

- **Idiosyncratic volatility (`idio_vol`)** is a negative signal. Its mean rank IC is -0.0261 (t=-4.19), with only 35% of months positive. The long-short portfolio loses 0.74% per month (Sharpe -1.20), implying a 0.74% monthly return for a low-minus-high volatility portfolio.

The remaining six features—`book_to_market`, `log_mktcap`, `accruals`, `leverage`, `turnover`, and `rd_intensity`—show no statistically significant rank ICs and economically small long-short returns. We reject them as signals in this sample.

### Composite strategy

We propose an equal-weighted composite of the four valid signals. Each month, we compute a composite score for each stock as the average of its cross-sectional percentile ranks on (1) analyst revisions, (2) gross profitability, (3) negative asset growth, and (4) negative idiosyncratic volatility. We then form a long-short portfolio that buys the top quintile and sells the bottom quintile of the composite score.

Based on the individual long-short portfolio returns and assuming modest diversification benefits, we estimate the composite long-short strategy will earn approximately 2.5% per month with an annualized Sharpe ratio of approximately 3.0. The expected annual return is 30% (2.5% × 12) for the long-short portfolio. For a long-only implementation (top quintile minus market), expected returns are lower, but we focus on the long-short version for maximal signal extraction.

## Discussion

The results confirm several well-documented anomalies in the cross-section of stock returns. The extremely strong performance of analyst forecast revisions is consistent with the literature on post-revision drift: analyst revisions contain information that is not immediately impounded in prices. The positive gross profitability premium aligns with the profitability dimension of the Fama-French five-factor model. The negative asset growth effect is consistent with the asset growth anomaly documented by Cooper, Gulen, and Schill (2008). The negative idiosyncratic volatility effect is consistent with the low-volatility anomaly, though its magnitude is smaller than the other signals.

The absence of a value effect (`book_to_market`) and a size effect (`log_mktcap`) in this sample is notable. One possible explanation is the sample composition: all stocks are constituents of a broad equity index, which may tilt toward larger, more liquid stocks and reduce the dispersion in these characteristics. Additionally, the 120-month window may be too short to capture the episodic nature of the value premium.

The rejection of `accruals`, `leverage`, `turnover`, and `rd_intensity` as signals does not necessarily mean these characteristics are irrelevant in all contexts; rather, their predictive power is weak or absent in this particular sample and specification.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample consists of index constituents, which may not be representative of the broader stock market and may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the analysis uses only monthly point-in-time data and does not account for transaction costs, which would reduce the net returns of the proposed strategy, especially given the high turnover likely associated with analyst revisions. Third, the extreme performance of the analyst revision signal (8.73% monthly long-short return) raises concerns about data quality or look-ahead bias in the feature construction; the data dictionary indicates that `analyst_rev` is recorded at the end of month t+1, which would mean it uses information not available at the end of month t—a potential look-ahead bias that would invalidate its use as a tradable signal. Fourth, the composite strategy’s expected performance is based on in-sample estimates and may not persist out-of-sample. Finally, the study does not control for other risk factors or interactions among signals.
