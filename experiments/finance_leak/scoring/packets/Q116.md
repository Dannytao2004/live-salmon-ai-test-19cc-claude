# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a Large-Cap Equity Panel: Evidence from Nine Accounting and Market Signals

## Abstract

We examine nine stock-level characteristics for their ability to predict next-month returns in a panel of 500 large-capitalization index constituents followed over 120 months (34,387 stock-month observations). Using monthly cross-sectional rank information coefficients (IC) and equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolios, we identify four signals with statistically meaningful predictive power: gross profitability (IC = 0.029, t = 3.83), idiosyncratic volatility (IC = −0.027, t = −3.76), leverage (IC = 0.014, t = 2.56), and accruals (IC = 0.013, t = 2.06). Asset growth is marginally significant (t = −1.87) and is treated as a weak, secondary signal. Book-to-market, log market cap, turnover, and R&D intensity show no reliable cross-sectional relationship with future returns. A composite long-short strategy combining the strongest signals yields an estimated annualized Sharpe ratio of roughly 1.4–1.6, though the modest per-signal ICs imply that implementation costs and capacity constraints are first-order concerns.

## Methods

**Data.** The panel contains monthly observations for the 500 stocks that were index constituents at the end of month 120, with history back to month 1 or index-inclusion date. Coverage grows from 139 stocks in month 1 to 500 by month 120, so early-sample cross-sections are thinner and noisier. All features are lagged accounting or market variables known at the end of month *t*; the target is the total return over month *t+1*.

**Signal evaluation.** For each feature we compute the monthly Spearman rank correlation with `ret_next` across stocks, then average across the 120 months to obtain the mean rank IC. Statistical significance is assessed with the IC t-statistic, mean / (std / √120), which accounts for month-to-month variation but not for cross-sectional correlation of residuals. We also report the fraction of months with positive IC.

**Portfolio construction.** For each feature, we form an equal-weighted long-short portfolio each month that buys the top quintile and shorts the bottom quintile, rebalancing monthly. Reported monthly returns and annualized Sharpe ratios use this construction. A composite strategy is formed by averaging the cross-sectional ranks of the signals judged valid, then taking the top-minus-bottom quintile spread.

**Signal selection rule.** We treat a feature as valid if |t-stat| ≥ ~2 and the sign of the long-short return is consistent with the IC sign. Features failing this bar, or whose economic interpretation is fragile, are rejected.

## Results

**Valid signals.** Gross profitability is the strongest single signal: mean IC = 0.0293 (t = 3.83), positive in 61% of months, with a long-short portfolio earning 0.88% per month and an annualized Sharpe of 1.19. High-gross-profit firms outperform, consistent with the profitability premium. Idiosyncratic volatility is the strongest negative signal: IC = −0.0266 (t = −3.76), positive in only 37% of months, long-short −0.78% per month, Sharpe −1.15; low-volatility stocks outperform, consistent with the low-volatility anomaly. Leverage has IC = 0.0137 (t = 2.56), long-short +0.50% per month, Sharpe 0.99. Accruals have IC = 0.0130 (t = 2.06), long-short +0.31% per month, Sharpe 0.50 — note the *positive* sign, opposite to the classic accrual anomaly, which is economically counterintuitive and suggests the result may be sample- or universe-specific. Asset growth has IC = −0.0164 (t = −1.87), long-short −0.64% per month, Sharpe −0.78; it is directionally consistent with the asset-growth anomaly but only marginally significant, so we treat it as a weak signal.

**Rejected signals.** Book-to-market (IC = 0.0029, t = 0.54), log market cap (IC = −0.0043, t = −0.67), turnover (IC = 0.0042, t = 0.64), and R&D intensity (IC = 0.0018, t = 0.30) all have t-statistics well below conventional thresholds and long-short Sharpes near zero; none provides reliable cross-sectional information in this large-cap universe.

**Composite strategy.** Averaging the ranks of gross profitability, idiosyncratic volatility (negatively signed), leverage, and accruals produces a diversified signal whose individual ICs are only weakly correlated by construction (accounting vs. market variables). Because combining four signals with individual Sharpes of roughly 1.2, −1.2, 1.0, and 0.5 typically raises the portfolio Sharpe relative to the best single signal — the low-volatility and profitability legs are the dominant contributors — we estimate a composite long-short Sharpe of approximately 1.4–1.6 and an annualized return of roughly 8–11% before costs.

## Discussion

The results are broadly consistent with the modern factor literature in a large-cap setting. Profitability and low volatility are the two robust dimensions; both have strong t-statistics, stable signs, and economically sensible interpretations. Leverage's positive IC is more surprising — high-leverage firms outperforming — and may reflect a risk premium for financial distress or a value/leverage interaction in this particular index. The positive accruals IC directly contradicts the well-documented negative accrual-return relation; in a sample restricted to surviving index constituents, survivorship and large-cap selection effects can easily flip or attenuate such anomalies. We therefore flag accruals as valid on statistical grounds but economically fragile.

The absence of a book-to-market effect is notable. In a universe of 500 surviving large-cap index members, value spreads are compressed and the value premium is known to be weak over many recent windows, so a near-zero IC is plausible rather than anomalous. Similarly, market cap has little cross-sectional power when the universe is already restricted to large caps, and turnover and R&D intensity carry little incremental information here.

The composite strategy's estimated Sharpe of 1.4–1.6 is attractive but must be read alongside its limitations: all ICs are small (|IC| < 0.03), so gross spreads are thin, and the long-short portfolios turn over monthly, generating transaction costs that can consume a substantial fraction of the gross return, especially in the low-volatility and accruals legs.

## Limitations

The analysis relies on summary statistics rather than raw data, so we cannot verify feature timing, winsorization, or missing-data treatment. The universe consists only of stocks that survived to month 120, introducing survivorship and look-ahead selection bias that likely inflates measured signal strength. Coverage grows from 139 to 500 names, so early-month ICs are estimated on small cross-sections and may be noisier. The IC t-statistics ignore cross-sectional correlation of returns, overstating significance when signals are correlated across stocks. The positive accruals sign contradicts prior literature and may not replicate out of sample. Reported returns and Sharpes are gross of transaction costs, borrow costs, and market impact. Finally, 120 months is a short sample for evaluating low-frequency anomalies, and the results may be specific to this index and period.
