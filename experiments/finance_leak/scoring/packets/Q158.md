# An Empirical Evaluation of Cross-Sectional Equity Signals in a Monthly Panel

## Abstract

We evaluate nine firm characteristics as predictors of next-month stock returns using a panel of 500 stocks over 120 months. Among the candidate signals, only leverage exhibits a statistically significant rank information coefficient (IC), with a mean monthly rank IC of 0.0164 and a t-statistic of 2.74. A long-short quintile strategy based on leverage delivers a monthly return of 0.52% and an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.92. The remaining eight characteristics fail to show reliable predictive power, with rank ICs near zero and t-statistics below conventional significance thresholds. We propose a simple long-short strategy that buys high-leverage stocks and sells low-leverage stocks, and we discuss economic interpretations and limitations of the findings.

## Methods

We analyze a monthly panel of 34,708 stock-month observations covering 500 unique stocks over 120 months. For each stock-month, nine firm characteristics are observed: book-to-market, gross profitability, log market capitalization, asset growth, accruals, idiosyncratic volatility, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity. The target variable is the stock's total return over the following month (`ret_next`).

To assess univariate predictive power, we compute the Spearman rank correlation between each characteristic and `ret_next` within each cross-section (month), then average these monthly rank correlations to obtain a mean rank IC. We also compute the standard deviation of monthly rank ICs and the associated t-statistic under the null hypothesis of zero mean IC. As an economic metric, we construct equal-weighted long-short portfolios that buy the top quintile and sell the bottom quintile of each characteristic each month, reporting the mean monthly return and annualized Sharpe ratio.

A signal is deemed valid if its rank IC t-statistic exceeds 2.0 in absolute value and its long-short Sharpe ratio exceeds 0.5, indicating both statistical reliability and economic relevance. Signals failing these criteria are rejected.

## Results

Table 1 summarizes the signal statistics. Among the nine features, only leverage meets the validity threshold. Leverage has a mean monthly rank IC of 0.0164 (t = 2.74), with positive ICs in 59% of months. The long-short quintile portfolio formed on leverage earns 0.52% per month on average, with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.92.

All other features have rank IC t-statistics below 1.2 in absolute value, and none exceeds a long-short Sharpe ratio of 0.74. Book-to-market, gross profitability, log market capitalization, turnover, and R&D intensity have rank ICs indistinguishable from zero (t-statistics below 0.3 in absolute value). Asset growth, accruals, and idiosyncratic volatility show slightly positive but statistically insignificant rank ICs, with t-statistics of 1.16, 1.03, and 0.86 respectively.

Based on these results, we propose a long-short equity strategy that buys the top quintile of stocks ranked by leverage and sells the bottom quintile, rebalanced monthly. The expected monthly return is approximately 0.52%, corresponding to an annualized return of about 6.4% before transaction costs. The annualized Sharpe ratio is estimated at 0.92.

## Discussion

The positive association between leverage and future returns is consistent with theories of risk-based return premia, where higher leverage reflects greater financial risk and thus commands higher expected returns. Alternatively, leverage may proxy for exposure to systematic distress risk or for firms' sensitivity to credit conditions. The economic magnitude of 0.52% per month is substantial but plausible for a univariate characteristic in a long-short context.

The failure of other widely studied characteristics—such as book-to-market, gross profitability, and asset growth—to predict returns in this sample is notable. This may reflect the relatively short sample period (120 months), the specific index-constituent universe, or the particular timing of annual accounting data availability. Idiosyncratic volatility, often negatively related to returns in prior literature, shows no significant relation here. The absence of a size effect (log market cap rank IC near zero) is also interesting and may be due to the index membership requirement imposed at the end of the sample.

The proposed leverage-based strategy is simple to implement and does not require complex modeling. However, its performance should be interpreted with caution given the multiple-testing context: with nine signals tested, a t-statistic of 2.74 corresponds to a p-value of approximately 0.006, which remains significant even after a Bonferroni correction for nine tests (adjusted threshold p < 0.0056), though only marginally.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample covers only 120 months and 500 stocks that are index constituents at the end of the sample, introducing potential survivorship and selection biases. Second, we do not account for transaction costs, market impact, or short-selling constraints, which would reduce the net performance of the long-short strategy. Third, the analysis is purely univariate; multivariate interactions and portfolio construction nuances are not considered. Fourth, the leverage signal may be collinear with other risk factors not measured here, and its predictive power may be time-varying. Finally, the multiple-testing concern, while partly mitigated, remains a caveat for the robustness of the single significant signal.
