# Report Q176

# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a Survivor Universe of 500 Index Constituents: Evidence from Nine Firm Characteristics

---

## Abstract

We examine nine accounting- and market-based firm characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month stock returns using a 120-month panel of 34,708 stock-month observations covering the 500 constituents of a broad equity index. Using monthly rank information coefficients (ICs) and equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolios, we find that most characteristics carry essentially no predictive power: only leverage exhibits a statistically meaningful rank IC (0.0164, t = 2.74), with all other features falling well below conventional significance thresholds. A long-short strategy that buys high-leverage stocks and sells low-leverage stocks earns 0.52% per month (6.4% annualized) with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.92, but this result is fragile given the survivor-universe construction and the small effect size. We conclude that the panel offers little reliable cross-sectional return signal, and that the one apparent signal is best interpreted as a leverage/risk-compensation effect rather than a robust alpha.

---

## Methods

**Data.** The panel consists of 34,708 monthly observations for 500 stocks over 120 months. The universe is defined as the set of stocks that were index constituents at the end of month 120, with history backfilled to month 1 or to the month of index inclusion. This is a *survivor universe*: firms that were delisted, acquired, or removed from the index before month 120 are absent. The number of stocks per month grows monotonically from 143 in month 1 to 500 in month 120, so early months are much thinner cross-sections.

**Features.** Nine characteristics are provided: book-to-market (`book_to_market`), gross profitability (`gross_prof`), size (`log_mktcap`), asset growth (`asset_growth`), accruals (`accruals`), idiosyncratic volatility (`idio_vol`), leverage (`leverage`), turnover (`turnover`), and R&D intensity (`rd_intensity`). All are observable at the end of month *t* and are used to predict the return over month *t+1* (`ret_next`), so the design is free of look-ahead bias by construction.

**Signal evaluation.** Two complementary statistics are used. First, the monthly rank IC (Spearman correlation between the feature and `ret_next` within each month), averaged across the 120 months. The t-statistic is computed as the mean IC divided by the cross-month standard deviation of the IC, scaled by √120. Second, an equal-weighted long-short portfolio that buys the top quintile and sells the bottom quintile of each feature each month; we report the mean monthly return and the annualized Sharpe ratio (monthly mean / monthly standard deviation × √12).

**Decision rule.** A feature is treated as a valid signal if |t-stat| ≥ 2 and the sign of the rank IC is consistent with the long-short portfolio direction. Features with |t-stat| < 2 are rejected as noise, regardless of the sign of the point estimate.

**Strategy construction.** For the surviving signal we form a monthly rebalanced, equal-weighted, dollar-neutral long-short portfolio (top vs. bottom quintile of the feature). We estimate expected performance directly from the reported long-short statistics, without leverage or transaction-cost adjustment, and note that costs and implementation frictions would reduce net returns.

## Results

### Signal-by-signal evidence

| Feature | Mean rank IC | IC t-stat | Months IC>0 | LS mean monthly | LS ann. Sharpe | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `leverage` | **0.0164** | **2.74** | 59% | **0.52%** | **0.92** | Valid |
| `accruals` | 0.0059 | 1.03 | 54% | 0.24% | 0.41 | Reject |
| `idio_vol` | 0.0056 | 0.86 | 50% | 0.13% | 0.19 | Reject |
| `asset_growth` | 0.0055 | 1.16 | 52% | 0.37% | 0.74 | Reject |
| `log_mktcap` | 0.0014 | 0.26 | 56% | −0.07% | −0.14 | Reject |
| `gross_prof` | 0.0012 | 0.23 | 50% | 0.12% | 0.22 | Reject |
| `rd_intensity` | 0.0010 | 0.20 | 52% | −0.06% | −0.11 | Reject |
| `book_to_market` | −0.0017 | −0.28 | 51% | −0.05% | −0.08 | Reject |
| `turnover` | −0.0005 | −0.10 | 52% | 0.13% | 0.23 | Reject |

**Leverage is the only feature that clears the significance bar.** Its rank IC of 0.0164 (t = 2.74) is positive, meaning higher-leverage stocks tend to outperform lower-leverage stocks next month. The corresponding equal-weighted long-short portfolio earns 0.52% per month, or roughly 6.4% annualized, with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.92. The IC is positive in 59% of months, the highest hit rate in the panel. Economically, this is consistent with a leverage/risk-premium interpretation: levered firms carry greater financial distress risk and earn a compensating return, and the effect is not obviously a value or size proxy since neither book-to-market nor log market cap shows any signal in this sample.

**Everything else is statistically indistinguishable from zero.** The next-largest t-statistics belong to `asset_growth` (1.16) and `accruals` (1.03); both have point estimates that would be interesting in the standard literature — asset growth negatively predicts returns (the asset-growth anomaly) and accruals negatively predict returns (the accruals anomaly) — but here both coefficients are *positive* and insignificant. That sign reversal is itself informative: in a survivor universe that excludes the small, distressed, and recently-listed firms where these anomalies are strongest, the effects vanish or flip. `idio_vol` (0.0056, t = 0.86) likewise fails the low-volatility-anomaly prediction. `book_to_market`, `gross_prof`, `rd_intensity`, and `turnover` are effectively zero, with |t| ≤ 0.28, and their long-short portfolios earn between −0.06% and +0.13% per month with Sharpe ratios between −0.11 and 0.23 — indistinguishable from noise.

### Strategy performance

A concentrated long-short portfolio on leverage — long the top quintile, short the bottom quintile, equal-weighted, monthly rebalanced, dollar-neutral — is the only strategy the data support. Expected annualized return is approximately **6.4%**, with expected annualized **Sharpe of 0.92**. This is a modest but non-trivial gross-of-cost result; after realistic transaction costs (turnover in a monthly quintile strategy is high) and shorting frictions, net Sharpe would likely fall into the 0.4–0.6 range. No multi-signal combination is justified, because combining eight noise features with one weak signal would primarily add estimation error.

---

## Discussion

The central finding is negative: eight of nine standard firm characteristics have no reliable cross-sectional predictive power for next-month returns in this panel. This is notable because several of these — accruals, asset growth, gross profitability, book-to-market — are canonical anomalies that are usually significant in broad CRSP/Compustat samples. Three explanations are most plausible.

First, **survivorship conditioning**. The universe is the set of stocks that survived to be index constituents at month 120. Survivors are disproportionately large, profitable, and stable firms. Anomalies are typically strongest in small, illiquid, financially distressed, and recently-listed stocks; screening those out mechanically attenuates the effects and can flip signs. The positive (rather than negative) accruals and asset-growth coefficients are consistent with this selection.

Second, **the panel is short and the cross-sections are thin early on**. With 120 months and as few as 143 stocks per month, the effective sample for detecting a rank IC of 0.01–0.02 is small. Only an effect as large as leverage's could clear the bar, and even that is close to the threshold.

Third, **the leverage result may itself be a risk or distress proxy rather than alpha**. High leverage is associated with high beta and distress risk; in a sample where the market has positive average returns, a high-beta long leg and low-beta short leg would mechanically produce a positive spread. The absence of a market-neutral control in the reported statistics means we cannot separate leverage-as-signal from leverage-as-beta. This is the most important caveat on the one positive finding.

Practically, the results argue against deploying a multi-factor composite on this panel. A single-factor leverage tilt is defensible only with beta hedging and careful cost accounting, and even then with modest expectations.

---

## Limitations

- **Survivor universe:** excluding delisted and removed firms biases the sample toward large, stable survivors and plausibly explains the absence of canonical anomalies.
- **No beta or factor neutralization:** the leverage long-short spread may reflect market-beta or distress-risk exposure rather than idiosyncratic alpha.
- **Thin early cross-sections:** months 1–60 have fewer than ~270 stocks, so rank ICs in the first half of the sample are noisier and may dominate the average.
- **No transaction costs or shorting frictions:** the reported 6.4% annual return and 0.92 Sharpe are gross; monthly quintile turnover would materially reduce net performance.
- **Single-market, single-horizon test:** only next-month returns are examined, so slower-moving signals (e.g., value, profitability) that pay off over quarters or years would be missed.
- **Multiple testing:** nine features were screened at |t| ≥ 2; with a 5% threshold, roughly one false positive is expected by chance, and leverage's t = 2.74 is not far above the cutoff.
- **No interaction or conditional analysis:** we cannot test whether signals work only within size, sector, or volatility buckets, which is where several of these characteristics are known to concentrate.
