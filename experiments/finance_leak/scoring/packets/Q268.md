## Title: An Evaluation of Equity Return Predictors in a Monthly Panel

## Abstract
We examine the cross-sectional predictive power of nine equity characteristics for next-month returns in a panel of 500 stocks over 120 months. Using rank correlation and quintile long-short portfolio tests, we find that only leverage exhibits a statistically significant positive relation with future returns, with a mean monthly rank IC of 0.0164 (t-stat = 2.74). All other characteristics—including value, profitability, size, and momentum-related variables—show economically small and statistically insignificant predictive power. A strategy that buys high-leverage and sells low-leverage stocks would have generated an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.92. These findings suggest that, in this dataset, leverage is the sole robust signal among the tested predictors.

## Methods
We use a panel of 34,708 stock-month observations spanning 120 months and 500 unique stocks. For each month, we compute the Spearman rank correlation (rank IC) between each feature and next-month returns (`ret_next`) across all stocks with available data. The mean rank IC is the time-series average of these monthly correlations, and the t-statistic is computed as the mean divided by the standard deviation of monthly ICs, multiplied by the square root of the number of months. A feature is considered a valid signal if its mean rank IC has an absolute t-statistic greater than 2.0 and a sign consistent with economic theory or prior literature. We also construct equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolios for each feature—buying the top quintile and selling the bottom quintile—and report the mean monthly return and annualized Sharpe ratio. Because the data are already summarized, no additional data cleaning or transformations are performed.

## Results
Table 1 presents the rank IC statistics and long-short portfolio performance for each feature. Among the nine characteristics, only `leverage` shows a statistically significant mean rank IC of 0.0164 (t-stat = 2.74), with 59% of months having a positive IC. The corresponding long-short portfolio earns an average monthly return of 0.52% with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.92, indicating a consistent positive relation between leverage and future returns. All other features have mean rank ICs close to zero and absolute t-statistics below 1.2. For example, `asset_growth` has the second-highest t-statistic at 1.16, but its long-short portfolio Sharpe ratio is only 0.74, and the IC sign is not reliably positive. `book_to_market`, `gross_prof`, `log_mktcap`, `accruals`, `idio_vol`, `turnover`, and `rd_intensity` all exhibit negligible predictive power, with long-short Sharpe ratios ranging from -0.14 to 0.41. Notably, `book_to_market` and `log_mktcap` show slightly negative long-short returns, contrary to traditional value and size premia, but these effects are statistically insignificant.

## Discussion
The positive and significant relation between leverage and future returns is consistent with the notion that higher-leverage firms are riskier and thus command a higher expected return, or that leverage proxies for financial distress risk. However, the economic magnitude is modest: a quintile long-short spread of 0.52% per month implies an annualized excess return of about 6.4% before costs. The absence of predictive power for other well-known anomalies—such as book-to-market, profitability, and asset growth—may reflect the specific sample composition (index constituents at the end of the sample period), survivorship bias, or the relatively short time span. The lack of a size effect is particularly noteworthy given the broad cross-section of market capitalizations. Overall, the results suggest that only leverage is a reliable predictor in this dataset, and a simple long-short leverage strategy would have delivered a Sharpe ratio near 1.0.

## Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, the dataset is a balanced panel of stocks that were index constituents at the end of month 120, which introduces survivorship and selection biases that may distort the true predictive relations. Second, the sample period is only 10 years (120 months), which limits statistical power and may not capture longer-term regime changes. Third, we do not account for transaction costs, liquidity constraints, or implementation frictions, which would reduce the net profitability of any strategy. Fourth, the features are based on annual report data that may be stale or subject to reporting lags, and we do not verify the timing of information availability. Finally, the analysis is purely cross-sectional and does not control for multiple testing; the single significant result for leverage could be a false discovery given the number of features tested.
