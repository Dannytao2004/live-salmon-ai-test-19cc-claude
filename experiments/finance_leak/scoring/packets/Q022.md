# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a Survivorship-Biased Index Panel: Weak Signals and the Dominance of Leverage

## Abstract

We examine nine accounting- and market-based characteristics for their ability to predict next-month cross-sectional stock returns in a 120-month panel of 500 index constituents. Using average monthly rank information coefficients (IC) and equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolios, we find that only one feature—leverage—exhibits a statistically meaningful relationship with future returns (mean rank IC = 0.0164, t = 2.74, long-short Sharpe = 0.92). The remaining eight features produce IC t-statistics below 1.2 and long-short Sharpes below 0.75, consistent with noise. We propose a leverage-based long-short strategy with an estimated annualized Sharpe near 0.9 and a gross annualized return of roughly 6%. All results are conditioned on a survivorship-biased universe of firms that survived to month 120, so they should be interpreted as in-sample associations rather than exploitable out-of-sample alpha.

## Methods

**Data.** The panel contains 34,708 stock-month observations covering 500 stocks over 120 months. The universe is defined as the set of stocks that are index constituents at the end of month 120, with history backfilled to their index entry date. This induces a survivorship bias: firms that were delisted, went bankrupt, or were removed from the index before month 120 are absent. The number of stocks per month grows monotonically from 143 to 500, confirming that the panel is a backward extension of a fixed terminal cross-section.

**Features.** Nine predictors are provided: book-to-market, gross profitability, log market cap, asset growth, accruals, idiosyncratic volatility, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity. All are constructed from information available at the end of month t (annual reports available at t, or trailing 3- to 60-day market data through t), so there is no look-ahead in the feature construction.

**Target.** `ret_next` is the total return over month t+1.

**Evaluation.** For each feature we use two complementary measures reported in the signal statistics: (i) the mean monthly rank IC (Spearman correlation between the feature and `ret_next` across stocks within a month, averaged over 120 months) with its t-statistic, and (ii) the mean monthly return and annualized Sharpe ratio of an equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolio that buys the top quintile and shorts the bottom quintile of the feature each month. We treat a feature as a valid signal only if it clears a conventional significance threshold (|t| > 2) and shows a directionally consistent long-short payoff. We deliberately hold the bar high given that multiple-testing across nine features inflates the probability of a spurious t-statistic above 2 to roughly 40% at the 5% level.

## Results

**Signal screen.** The signal statistics table is reproduced in summary form below.

| Feature | Mean rank IC | IC t-stat | Months IC>0 | L/S monthly ret | L/S ann. Sharpe |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| book_to_market | -0.0017 | -0.28 | 51% | -0.05% | -0.08 |
| gross_prof | 0.0012 | 0.23 | 50% | 0.12% | 0.22 |
| log_mktcap | 0.0014 | 0.26 | 56% | -0.07% | -0.14 |
| asset_growth | 0.0055 | 1.16 | 52% | 0.37% | 0.74 |
| accruals | 0.0059 | 1.03 | 54% | 0.24% | 0.41 |
| idio_vol | 0.0056 | 0.86 | 50% | 0.13% | 0.19 |
| **leverage** | **0.0164** | **2.74** | **59%** | **0.52%** | **0.92** |
| turnover | -0.0005 | -0.10 | 52% | 0.13% | 0.23 |
| rd_intensity | 0.0010 | 0.20 | 52% | -0.06% | -0.11 |

Only leverage clears the significance bar. Its mean rank IC of 0.0164 is economically small but statistically robust (t = 2.74), and the sign is positive: higher-leverage firms subsequently outperform lower-leverage firms. The hit rate (59% of months with positive IC) is consistent with a persistent, if modest, effect. The long-short portfolio earns 0.52% per month (≈6.4% annualized gross) with an annualized Sharpe of 0.92.

**Near-misses.** Asset growth (t = 1.16) and accruals (t = 1.03) show the expected signs—asset growth positively predicts returns and accruals positively predict returns—but neither is statistically distinguishable from zero. Notably, the accruals sign is opposite to the classic Sloan (1996) finding, which we attribute to the survivorship-biased universe and to the fact that accruals here are scaled by total assets from annual reports rather than being a trading signal constructed in real time.

**Non-signals.** Book-to-market, gross profitability, log market cap, idiosyncratic volatility, turnover, and R&D intensity all have |t| < 0.9 and long-short Sharpes below 0.25. Their IC signs are also unstable: book-to-market is negative, log market cap is positive, and turnover is essentially zero. These are indistinguishable from noise in this sample.

**Strategy.** We propose a monthly equal-weighted long-short portfolio: long the top quintile of leverage, short the bottom quintile, rebalanced at each month-end. Based on the reported long-short mean of 0.52% per month and Sharpe of 0.92, we estimate a gross annualized return of approximately 6.4% and an annualized Sharpe of approximately 0.9. After realistic transaction costs (assume 10–20 bps round-trip per name per month on a ~100-name book), net annualized return falls to roughly 3–5%, and the Sharpe to roughly 0.5–0.7.

## Discussion

The central finding is that, in this panel, most of the canonical equity characteristics fail to predict next-month returns, and the one feature that does—leverage—does so with a sign that runs against the standard "leverage is risky, therefore low-leverage firms earn higher returns" intuition from the betting-against-beta and financial-distress literatures. Two explanations are plausible. First, the universe is conditioned on survival to month 120. High-leverage firms that would have failed are absent, so the surviving high-leverage names are disproportionately those that successfully managed their debt—a selection effect that mechanically induces a positive leverage-return relation. Second, the sample is a single broad index of 500 names, so much of the cross-sectional dispersion in returns is idiosyncratic and the effective number of independent bets per month is modest.

The broader lesson is about statistical discipline. With nine features tested, a t-statistic of 2.74 is the only one that survives even the loosest multiple-testing correction; the next-largest t (1.16) is well within what one expects from noise. The long-short Sharpes tell the same story: 0.92 for leverage versus 0.74, 0.41, and 0.23 for the runners-up, and near zero for the rest. A researcher who picked the best of nine features by in-sample Sharpe would be overfitting; the fact that leverage is also the best by IC and by hit rate provides some, but not decisive, corroboration.

## Limitations

- **Survivorship bias.** The universe consists only of firms that survived to month 120; this mechanically distorts leverage, distress, and accrual relationships and likely inflates the leverage signal.
- **Single universe, single period.** 500 stocks over 120 months is a small effective sample for cross-sectional inference; results may not generalize to other indices, countries, or eras.
- **No out-of-sample test.** All statistics are in-sample averages; there is no holdout period, so the reported Sharpe is an upper bound on realizable performance.
- **Transaction costs and capacity.** The strategy trades roughly 200 names per month; the reported 6.4% gross return is before costs, and net performance could be materially lower.
- **Feature construction ambiguity.** Annual-report features are updated only when a new report is available, so effective sample sizes per feature are smaller than the row count suggests, and the exact timing convention is not verifiable from the summary alone.
- **Multiple testing.** Nine features were screened; a t-statistic of 2.74 corresponds to a p-value near 0.007, which is suggestive but not overwhelming after accounting for the number of tests.
