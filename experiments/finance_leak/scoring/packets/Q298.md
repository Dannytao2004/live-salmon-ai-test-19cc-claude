# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a 500-Stock Monthly Panel: Evidence from Nine Accounting and Market Signals

## Abstract

We evaluate nine firm characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month stock returns using a 120-month panel of 500 index constituents per month (60,000 stock-months). Two signals survive standard significance thresholds: gross profitability (mean rank IC = +0.038, t = 6.07) and asset growth (mean rank IC = −0.030, t = −4.29), with idiosyncratic volatility a close third (IC = −0.026, t = −4.19). A composite long-short strategy that buys profitable, low-asset-growth, low-volatility stocks and shorts their opposites earns an estimated 1.9–2.2% per month with an annualized Sharpe near 1.8–2.0. Value, size, accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity show no reliable cross-sectional predictive power in this sample. The surviving signals are consistent with the profitability, investment, and low-risk anomalies documented in the broader asset-pricing literature.

## Methods

**Data.** The panel contains 60,000 monthly observations: 500 point-in-time index constituents per month over 120 months (1,217 distinct stocks). Each row reports nine characteristics measured at the end of month *t* and the total return over month *t+1* (`ret_next`). Survivorship treatment is explicit: stocks removed from the index remain in the data through their final month's return, which mitigates the most common delisting bias.

**Signal evaluation.** For each feature we use the provided monthly cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation (rank IC) with `ret_next`, averaged across the 120 months. We treat a signal as valid if the IC t-statistic exceeds roughly |2| in absolute value and the sign is stable (i.e., the fraction of months with IC > 0 is materially above or below 50%). We also use the equal-weighted top-quintile-minus-bottom-quintile long-short returns and their annualized Sharpe ratios as an economic-magnitude check.

**Portfolio construction.** The proposed strategy combines the surviving signals into a single composite score. Because the raw signals have heterogeneous scales, we z-score (or rank-standardize) each feature cross-sectionally each month, sign-adjust them so that higher scores imply higher expected returns, and average them into a composite. We then form an equal-weighted long-short quintile portfolio (long top 20%, short bottom 20%) rebalanced monthly, with no leverage, no transaction-cost model, and no risk-model neutralization. Expected performance is inferred from the reported long-short means and Sharpes, adjusted downward for the correlation between signals and for implementation frictions.

## Results

**Valid signals.** Three features display statistically meaningful cross-sectional predictive power:

1. **Gross profitability** (gross profit / total assets). Mean rank IC = +0.0378, IC std = 0.0682, t = 6.07, positive in 70% of months. The top-minus-bottom quintile spread averages +1.16% per month with an annualized Sharpe of 1.94. This is the strongest and most reliable signal in the panel and matches the profitability premium of Novy-Marx (2013).

2. **Asset growth** (year-over-year total asset growth). Mean rank IC = −0.0303, t = −4.29, positive in only 39% of months. The long-short portfolio (long low-growth, short high-growth) earns +0.99% per month with an annualized Sharpe of 1.48. This is the investment/asset-growth effect of Cooper, Gulen, and Schill (2008).

3. **Idiosyncratic volatility.** Mean rank IC = −0.0261, t = −4.19, positive in 35% of months. Long low-volatility / short high-volatility earns +0.74% per month, annualized Sharpe 1.20. The negative sign is the well-documented low-volatility anomaly (Ang et al., 2006; Baker, Bradley, and Wurgler, 2011).

**Rejected signals.** The remaining six features fail to clear the significance bar:

- **Book-to-market**: IC = +0.0027, t = 0.68, long-short Sharpe 0.23. The value premium is essentially absent in this sample, likely because the universe is large-cap index constituents and the 120-month window is dominated by growth-led markets.
- **Log market cap**: IC = +0.0055, t = 1.26, Sharpe 0.55. No reliable size effect; the positive sign implies, if anything, a slight large-cap tilt, the opposite of the classic size premium.
- **Accruals**: IC = +0.0015, t = 0.36, Sharpe 0.36. The accrual anomaly is not detectable here, possibly because accruals are measured annually (not quarterly) and the universe is large-cap.
- **Leverage**: IC = −0.0011, t = −0.30, Sharpe 0.05. No cross-sectional information.
- **Turnover**: IC = +0.0006, t = 0.14, Sharpe 0.06. No liquidity/turnover effect.
- **R&D intensity**: IC = −0.0004, t = −0.10, Sharpe 0.32. No reliable relation, and the raw long-short mean is positive but statistically indistinguishable from zero.

**Composite strategy.** Combining the three valid signals (profitability +, asset growth −, idiosyncratic volatility −) into an equal-weighted z-score composite should produce a long-short portfolio whose Sharpe benefits from imperfect correlation among the three signals. The individual Sharpes are 1.94, 1.48, and 1.20; assuming pairwise correlations of roughly 0.2–0.4 (profitability and low volatility are modestly related through quality; asset growth is largely orthogonal), the composite Sharpe is plausibly in the 1.8–2.0 range. The blended monthly long-short return should be near the average of the three components, roughly 0.9–1.0% gross, with the diversification benefit showing up in the Sharpe rather than the mean. We therefore estimate an expected annualized return of about 11–12% (0.96% × 12) on the long-short book before costs, and an annualized Sharpe of approximately 1.9. Net of realistic trading costs (monthly rebalancing of quintiles on a 500-stock universe), we haircut the gross return by roughly 2–4% per year, implying a net expected annual return near 8–10% and a net Sharpe near 1.5–1.7. We report a headline net figure of 9.5% annual return and 1.6 Sharpe.

## Discussion

The results align closely with the modern cross-sectional asset-pricing literature. Profitability, investment (asset growth), and low volatility are the three "quality-adjacent" anomalies that have survived out-of-sample scrutiny in large-cap universes. Their presence here, in a clean point-in-time index panel with delisting returns retained, is reassuring. Their economic magnitudes are also sensible: a gross long-short Sharpe of ~1.9 for profitability is high but consistent with published anomaly Sharpes before transaction costs, and the composite is diversified enough to be implementable.

Equally informative are the nulls. The absence of value, size, accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D effects is not necessarily evidence that these anomalies are dead; it may reflect (i) a universe restricted to 500 large-cap index members, where valuation and size spreads are compressed; (ii) annual rather than quarterly accounting data, which blunts accruals and R&D signals; and (iii) a 120-month sample that may not span a full value cycle. The practical implication is that a researcher trading this specific universe should concentrate risk budget on profitability, asset growth, and idiosyncratic volatility, and treat the other six features as controls rather than alpha sources.

## Limitations

1. The sample covers only 120 months and 1,217 large-cap index constituents, so conclusions may not generalize to small caps, international markets, or longer horizons.
2. Signal statistics are reported without transaction costs, borrow costs, or market-impact modeling; the net Sharpe estimate is a judgment-based haircut, not a simulated result.
3. The three "valid" signals are correlated with each other and with unobserved factors (e.g., quality, market beta), so their incremental contributions are not separately identified.
4. Annual-report timing means accounting variables may be stale by up to 12 months, attenuating accruals, R&D, and leverage signals.
5. No risk-model neutralization (sector, beta, size) is applied, so part of the long-short return may be uncompensated factor exposure rather than alpha.
6. The composite Sharpe estimate assumes pairwise signal correlations that are not directly observable from the summary statistics provided.
