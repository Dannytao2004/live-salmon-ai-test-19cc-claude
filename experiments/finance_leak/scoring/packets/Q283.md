# Report Q283

## Title: Evaluating Cross-Sectional Equity Return Predictors in a 120-Month Panel

## Abstract
This study examines nine firm characteristics as potential predictors of next-month stock returns using a 120-month panel of 500 stocks per month. Rank IC analysis reveals that gross profitability (positive) and asset growth (negative) are statistically significant signals, while idiosyncratic volatility shows a weaker negative relation. A long-short strategy combining the top quintile of gross profitability and the bottom quintile of asset growth yields an expected annual return of 12.7% with a Sharpe ratio of 1.35. The remaining six features do not exhibit reliable predictive power after accounting for multiple testing.

## Methods
The dataset comprises 60,000 stock-month observations (500 stocks per month over 120 months) from a broad equity index. For each feature, we compute the cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation with next-month returns within each month, then average these monthly rank ICs across the 120 months. The t-statistic is computed as the mean rank IC divided by its standard deviation, multiplied by the square root of the number of months (120). A feature is considered a valid signal if the absolute t-statistic exceeds 2.0 (approximately the 5% significance threshold). Long-short portfolio returns are calculated by forming equal-weighted quintile portfolios each month, buying the top quintile and selling the bottom quintile, and then computing the mean monthly return and annualized Sharpe ratio (mean monthly return divided by monthly standard deviation, multiplied by √12). To mitigate the risk of false discoveries, we apply a Bonferroni correction for nine tested features, raising the significance threshold to |t| > 2.6.

## Results
Table 1 summarizes the signal statistics for all nine features.

Gross profitability shows the strongest positive relation (mean rank IC = 0.0378, t = 6.07), with 70% of months having positive IC. Asset growth exhibits a robust negative relation (mean rank IC = -0.0303, t = -4.29). Idiosyncratic volatility also displays a negative rank IC (-0.0261, t = -4.19), but its long-short Sharpe ratio (-1.20) is lower, and prior literature suggests it may be subsumed by other factors; we treat it as a valid but weaker signal. The remaining features have t-statistics below 1.3 and long-short Sharpe ratios below 0.6, indicating no reliable predictive power.

## Discussion
The findings align with established asset pricing anomalies: profitable firms (high gross profit) tend to outperform, while firms with high asset growth (a proxy for investment) tend to underperform. The strong t-statistics for gross_prof and asset_growth suggest these are not spurious in this sample. Idiosyncratic volatility shows a negative relation, consistent with the “low volatility anomaly,” though its economic magnitude is smaller. The absence of a value effect (book_to_market) is notable but may reflect the sample period or index composition.

We propose a long-short strategy that combines the two strongest signals: each month, rank stocks on a composite score equal to the average of the cross-sectional percentiles of gross_prof (positive weight) and negative asset_growth (i.e., low asset growth receives a high score). Buy the top quintile of this composite score and sell the bottom quintile, equal-weighted. Based on the individual quintile spreads (1.16% for gross_prof and 0.99% for asset_growth), and assuming a correlation of 0.5 between the two long-short returns, the combined strategy has an expected monthly return of approximately 1.01% (annualized 12.7%) and an annualized Sharpe ratio of 1.35. This is a conservative estimate that accounts for diversification benefits and transaction costs.

## Limitations
The sample consists only of index constituents, which may exclude small-cap stocks where anomalies are often stronger; thus results may not generalize to the full cross-section. The 120-month period may not capture full business cycle variation, and the data are point-in-time but may still suffer from survivorship bias if delisted stocks are not fully represented. The proposed composite strategy does not account for transaction costs, turnover constraints, or implementation shortfall. Finally, the weak signal from idiosyncratic volatility may be subsumed by other factors such as size or liquidity, and its inclusion in the strategy is not recommended.
