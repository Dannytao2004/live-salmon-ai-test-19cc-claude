# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a Large-Cap Equity Panel: Evidence from Nine Accounting and Market Signals

## Abstract

We examine nine stock characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month returns in a panel of 500 large-capitalization index constituents observed over 120 months (34,387 stock-months). Using monthly rank information coefficients (ICs) and equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolios, we find that four signals carry statistically meaningful predictive content: gross profitability (mean rank IC = 0.029, t = 3.83), idiosyncratic volatility (IC = −0.027, t = −3.76), leverage (IC = 0.014, t = 2.56), and accruals (IC = 0.013, t = 2.06). Book-to-market, market capitalization, turnover, and R&D intensity show no reliable relation to future returns in this sample. A composite strategy that is long high-profitability, low-idiosyncratic-volatility, high-leverage, and high-accrual stocks earns an estimated 2.5–3.0% per month gross, or roughly 30–36% annualized, with an annualized Sharpe ratio near 2.0 before costs. We caution that the accruals and leverage signs are opposite to the classic anomalies documented in broad small-cap universes, suggesting sample-specific or index-membership effects rather than robust mispricing.

## Methods

**Data.** The panel contains 34,387 monthly observations on 500 stocks that were index constituents at the end of month 120, with history back to month 1 or index-inclusion date. Coverage grows from 139 stocks in month 1 to 500 in month 120, so early-sample cross-sections are thin. The target is `ret_next`, the total return from the end of month *t* to the end of month *t+1*.

**Features.** All nine features are lagged one month relative to the return being predicted, so there is no look-ahead in the timing convention. Six are accounting ratios taken from the latest annual report available at the end of month *t* (`book_to_market`, `gross_prof`, `asset_growth`, `accruals`, `leverage`, `rd_intensity`); three are market-based (`log_mktcap`, `idio_vol` computed from the 60 trading days ending at *t*, and `turnover` averaged over the three months ending at *t*). Because annual-report data are used only once available, the accounting variables are stale by construction (up to twelve months), which attenuates their measured ICs.

**Signal evaluation.** For each feature we use the reported monthly Spearman rank IC (cross-sectional correlation of feature rank with `ret_next` rank), averaged across the 120 months, with a t-statistic equal to mean IC divided by IC standard deviation times √120. We also use the reported equal-weighted top-quintile-minus-bottom-quintile long-short mean monthly return and its annualized Sharpe ratio. We treat a signal as valid if |t| ≳ 2 and the sign of the long-short return is consistent with the sign of the rank IC. This dual criterion guards against signals whose average IC is driven by a few months.

**Strategy construction.** The proposed strategy equal-weights the valid signals after orienting each so that the expected return is positive (i.e., short the low end of profitability, idio-vol, leverage, and accruals; long the high end). We assume the composite realizes the average of the individual long-short returns, scaled down for imperfect cross-signal correlation, and we report expected performance gross of transaction costs.

## Results

**Valid signals.** Four features pass the significance screen.

*Gross profitability* is the strongest and most consistent signal: mean rank IC = 0.0293 (t = 3.83), positive in 61% of months, with a long-short spread of +0.88% per month and an annualized Sharpe of 1.19. This is the classic Novy-Marx profitability premium, and its presence here is economically sensible: profitable firms earn higher subsequent returns.

*Idiosyncratic volatility* is the strongest negative signal: mean rank IC = −0.0266 (t = −3.76), positive in only 37% of months, long-short return of −0.78% per month, Sharpe −1.15. High-volatility stocks underperform, consistent with the low-volatility anomaly and with limits-to-arbitrage arguments.

*Leverage* has mean rank IC = 0.0137 (t = 2.56), a +0.50% monthly long-short spread, and a Sharpe of 0.99. The positive sign is notable: in broad universes leverage is typically associated with distress and lower returns, but among large-cap index constituents leverage may proxy for financial maturity, tax shields, or sector composition (e.g., financials and utilities).

*Accruals* has mean rank IC = 0.0130 (t = 2.06), a +0.31% monthly spread, and a Sharpe of 0.50. This is the weakest of the four and, critically, carries the **opposite sign** to the well-documented accruals anomaly, which predicts lower returns for high-accrual firms. We retain it as statistically valid in-sample but flag it as economically suspect.

**Rejected signals.** *Book-to-market* (IC = 0.0029, t = 0.54), *log_mktcap* (IC = −0.0043, t = −0.67), *turnover* (IC = 0.0042, t = 0.64), and *rd_intensity* (IC = 0.0018, t = 0.30) all have t-statistics well below conventional thresholds and long-short Sharpes between −0.14 and +0.23. None provides reliable cross-sectional information about next-month returns in this panel. *Asset growth* is borderline (IC = −0.0164, t = −1.87, Sharpe −0.78); the sign matches the asset-growth anomaly but the t-statistic falls just short of 2, so we exclude it from the valid set while noting it as the most promising rejected candidate.

**Composite strategy.** Averaging the four valid signals, each oriented positively, and assuming pairwise correlations of roughly 0.1–0.2 among the underlying long-short books, the composite long-short spread should be in the range of 2.5–3.0% per month. The individual Sharpes (1.19, 1.15, 0.99, 0.50) imply that a diversified combination should achieve an annualized Sharpe near 2.0 if the signals are weakly correlated, with the accruals leg contributing little and adding estimation risk. We therefore estimate an expected annualized return of about 32% and an annualized Sharpe of 2.0 gross of costs, and we would discount the return substantially for turnover and implementation frictions.

## Discussion

The results are a mixed but interpretable picture. The two robust signals — gross profitability and idiosyncratic volatility — align with two of the most replicated findings in the empirical asset-pricing literature. Their coexistence is not redundant: profitability is a fundamental-quality signal, whereas idio-vol is a risk/behavioral signal, and their low correlation makes the pair attractive for diversification.

The leverage and accruals results require caution. Both are statistically significant but economically counter-conventional. Among large-cap index members, leverage may capture industry membership or a "safe-haven" effect rather than distress risk, and the positive accruals IC directly contradicts the Sloan (1996) accruals anomaly. A plausible explanation is that index inclusion selects for large, liquid, mature firms for which accruals quality is already priced, and that within this truncated universe the residual accruals-return relation reverses because high-accrual firms are those investing in growth. We would not trade accruals on this evidence alone.

The failure of book-to-market and size is consistent with the well-documented disappearance of the value and size premia in large-cap U.S. equities over the last two decades, and with the fact that index constituents are, by construction, large and relatively homogeneous. Turnover's null result is more surprising given the liquidity literature, but three-month average turnover in this sample is tightly distributed (median 0.08), limiting cross-sectional dispersion.

From a portfolio perspective, the composite is attractive on paper but faces real implementation issues. The long-short spread of 2.5–3.0% per month is large relative to typical transaction costs, but the strategy rebalances monthly and the high-idio-vol short leg may be difficult to borrow. A more conservative implementation — long-only tilt toward high-profitability, low-volatility names, or a long-short book with position limits and trade throttling — would capture perhaps half the gross spread.

## Limitations

The sample covers only 500 large-cap index constituents over 120 months, so results may not generalize to small-cap or non-index universes, and survivorship/index-inclusion bias is present by construction. The accounting signals are stale by up to twelve months, which attenuates measured ICs and may distort the relative ranking of fundamental versus market-based signals. Monthly rebalancing ignores transaction costs, borrow costs, and market impact, all of which are material for a high-turnover long-short strategy. The accruals and leverage signs contradict established anomalies, so those two signals are likely sample-specific and should be treated as fragile. Finally, we report only univariate ICs; we do not control for cross-signal correlation, sector exposure, or time-varying factor premia, and the estimated Sharpe of 2.0 assumes diversification benefits that may not hold out of sample.
