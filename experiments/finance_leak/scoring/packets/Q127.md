# Cross-Sectional Equity Return Signals in a Monthly Index Panel: Profitability, Investment, and Low-Volatility Effects

## Abstract
I analyze a 120-month panel of 500 index constituents per month, with nine stock characteristics and next-month returns. Using monthly Spearman rank ICs and equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolios, I find three robust signals: gross profitability is positively predictive, while asset growth and idiosyncratic volatility are negatively predictive. The remaining six characteristics—book-to-market, log market cap, accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity—do not show statistically reliable cross-sectional predictive power in this sample. A composite strategy that buys high-gross-profitability, low-asset-growth, and low-idiosyncratic-volatility stocks and shorts the opposite quintiles earns an estimated 0.96% per month, or about 12.2% annualized before costs, with an estimated Sharpe ratio near 2.0.

## Methods
The data contain 60,000 stock-month observations from months 1 to 120, with 500 index constituents per month. The target is `ret_next`, the total return over month t+1. All features are point-in-time at month t: accounting variables (`book_to_market`, `gross_prof`, `asset_growth`, `accruals`, `leverage`, `rd_intensity`) come from the latest annual report available at the end of month t; market and trading variables (`log_mktcap`, `idio_vol`, `turnover`) are measured at or over trailing windows ending at month t. Thus, no feature uses information from month t+1.

I evaluate each signal using three statistics reported in the panel: the average monthly Spearman rank IC between the feature and `ret_next`, its t-statistic across 120 months, and the mean monthly return and annualized Sharpe ratio of an equal-weighted long-short quintile portfolio. I treat a feature as valid if it has a consistent sign, a t-statistic greater than 2 in absolute value, and an economically plausible interpretation. For the strategy, I orient each valid signal so that the long leg is expected to outperform: high `gross_prof`, low `asset_growth`, and low `idio_vol`. I then estimate a combined equal-weighted long-short portfolio.

## Results
Three features pass the validity criteria.

| Feature | Direction | Mean rank IC | IC t-stat | Long-short monthly return | Long-short annualized Sharpe |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| gross_prof | Positive | 0.0378 | 6.07 | 1.16% | 1.94 |
| asset_growth | Negative | -0.0303 | -4.29 | -0.99% | -1.48 |
| idio_vol | Negative | -0.0261 | -4.19 | -0.74% | -1.20 |

`gross_prof` has the strongest signal: its rank IC is 0.0378, positive in 70% of months, and its high-minus-low quintile portfolio earns 1.16% per month with a Sharpe ratio of 1.94. `asset_growth` is reliably negative: high-growth firms underperform low-growth firms by 0.99% per month in the high-minus-low quintile, with a t-statistic of -4.29. `idio_vol` is also negative: high-volatility stocks underperform low-volatility stocks by 0.74% per month, with a t-statistic of -4.19.

The other six features are rejected. `book_to_market` has a rank IC of 0.0027 (t = 0.68), `log_mktcap` has 0.0055 (t = 1.26), `accruals` has 0.0015 (t = 0.36), `leverage` has -0.0011 (t = -0.30), `turnover` has 0.0006 (t = 0.14), and `rd_intensity` has -0.0004 (t = -0.10). Their long-short returns and Sharpes are also close to zero, so they do not provide reliable standalone return predictions in this panel.

For the composite strategy, I equal-weight the three valid signals after orienting them in the predicted direction. The average monthly return is approximately (1.16% + 0.99% + 0.74%) / 3 = 0.96% per month. Compounded over 12 months, this implies an annualized return of about 12.2%. Assuming moderate positive correlation among the three sleeves, the combined Sharpe ratio is estimated near 2.0, before transaction costs.

## Discussion
The results align with three well-known cross-sectional equity anomalies. The positive `gross_prof` signal is consistent with a profitability premium: firms with high gross profits relative to assets tend to generate stronger future returns, perhaps because profitability is mispriced or because it proxies for higher-quality fundamentals. The negative `asset_growth` signal matches the investment anomaly: firms that expand their asset base aggressively tend to earn lower subsequent returns, consistent with overinvestment or q-theory explanations. The negative `idio_vol` signal reflects the low-volatility anomaly: high-idiosyncratic-volatility stocks underperform, possibly because investors overpay for lottery-like exposure or because leverage-constrained investors bid up high-beta/high-volatility names.

The rejected signals are also informative. Value, size, accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity show no reliable predictive power in this index-constituent universe. This may reflect the large-cap, index-member sample, which excludes many microcaps and distressed names where value and accrual effects are often strongest. It may also reflect the 120-month sample period, which may not contain the same regime variation as longer historical studies. Finally, because accounting variables are annual and point-in-time lagged, their information may already be incorporated into prices by the time they are observable.

## Limitations
The sample covers only 120 months and 500 index constituents per month, so results may not generalize to broader universes or longer periods. The analysis does not account for transaction costs, market impact, borrow costs, or shorting constraints, which would reduce the implementable composite return. The strategy uses equal-weighted quintiles and does not neutralize industry, sector, or other risk exposures. The signals may be correlated with each other and with unobserved risk factors, so the estimated Sharpe ratio is not a risk-adjusted alpha. Accounting variables are annual, stale for up to a year, and available only at a lag, limiting the timeliness of fundamental signals. The reported ICs and long-short returns are in-sample statistics and may reflect multiple testing or data-mining bias.
