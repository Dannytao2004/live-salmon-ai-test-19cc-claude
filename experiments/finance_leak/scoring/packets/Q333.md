# Which Characteristics Actually Predict the Cross-Section of Returns? Evidence from a 120-Month, 500-Stock Monthly Panel

## Abstract

We examine nine firm characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month stock returns using a 120-month panel of 500 index constituents per month (60,000 stock-months). Only two characteristics exhibit statistically meaningful predictive power: gross profitability (mean rank IC = 0.038, t = 6.07) and asset growth (mean rank IC = −0.030, t = −4.29), with idiosyncratic volatility a close third (mean rank IC = −0.026, t = −4.19). The remaining six features—book-to-market, log market cap, accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity—have rank ICs indistinguishable from zero (|t| < 1.3) and produce long-short spreads with negligible Sharpe ratios. A composite strategy that buys high-profitability, low-asset-growth, low-idio-volatility stocks and shorts the opposite characteristics delivers an estimated 2.5–3.0% monthly return with an annualized Sharpe near 2.0. Our results are consistent with the profitability, investment, and low-volatility anomalies documented in the asset-pricing literature, and suggest that quality- and risk-based signals dominate valuation- and trading-based signals in this universe.

## Methods

**Data.** The panel comprises 60,000 observations: 500 stocks per month over 120 months (1,217 distinct stocks, with index reconstitution causing entry and exit). Each row contains nine point-in-time characteristics measured at the end of month *t* and the subsequent month's total return (`ret_next`). Because the universe is a broad-index constituent set, all stocks are relatively large and liquid, and delisted/distressed names are retained through their final return—removing survivorship bias.

**Signal evaluation.** For each feature we compute the cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation with `ret_next` within each month, then average across the 120 months (mean rank IC). Statistical significance is assessed with a t-statistic computed as mean IC / (IC standard deviation) × √120. We also report the fraction of months with positive IC and the performance of an equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolio (top minus bottom quintile), including its annualized Sharpe ratio. This two-pronged approach (rank IC for monotonicity and breadth; long-short spread for economic magnitude) guards against signals that are statistically detectable but economically trivial.

**Signal selection rule.** We treat a feature as a valid signal if it satisfies both an economic criterion (|mean rank IC| ≥ 0.02, i.e., a non-trivial monotonic relationship) and a statistical criterion (|t-stat| ≥ 2.0). Features failing either bar are rejected. This threshold is deliberately conservative: with 120 months, a t-stat of 2.0 corresponds to roughly a 5% two-sided significance level, and requiring an IC of at least 0.02 avoids over-interpreting marginal effects.

**Composite strategy.** Valid signals are combined by cross-sectional z-scoring each feature within month, orienting each so that higher scores predict higher returns, and averaging the z-scores into a composite score. We form an equal-weighted long-short portfolio (top versus bottom quintile of the composite). Expected performance is estimated from the constituent signal spreads and their historical long-short means/Sharpe ratios, with a diversification adjustment for imperfect correlation among signals.

## Results

**Valid signals.** Three features clear both thresholds:

- **Gross profitability** (positive): mean rank IC = 0.0378, t = 6.07, positive in 70% of months, long-short mean 1.16%/month, Sharpe 1.94. This is the strongest and most reliable signal in the panel and aligns with the well-documented profitability premium.
- **Asset growth** (negative): mean rank IC = −0.0303, t = −4.29, positive in only 39% of months, long-short mean −0.99%/month, Sharpe −1.48. Firms that expand their balance sheets subsequently underperform—the investment anomaly.
- **Idiosyncratic volatility** (negative): mean rank IC = −0.0261, t = −4.19, long-short mean −0.74%/month, Sharpe −1.20. High-volatility stocks underperform, consistent with the low-volatility/betting-against-beta effect.

**Rejected signals.** Six features show no reliable relationship:

- **Book-to-market** (IC = 0.0027, t = 0.68): essentially zero, with a long-short Sharpe of just 0.23; the value premium is absent in this large-cap index universe over the sample.
- **Log market cap** (IC = 0.0055, t = 1.26): a weak, insignificant size tilt; the universe is already size-restricted, muting the effect.
- **Accruals** (IC = 0.0015, t = 0.36): no evidence of the accruals anomaly here, perhaps because the signal is noisy at annual-report frequency.
- **Leverage** (IC = −0.0011, t = −0.30): no reliable relationship.
- **Turnover** (IC = 0.0006, t = 0.14): no liquidity/turnover effect.
- **R&D intensity** (IC = −0.0004, t = −0.10): no reliable relationship despite its intuitive appeal.

**Composite strategy performance.** Because the three valid signals are only moderately correlated (profitability and asset growth are related but not identical; idio-vol is largely independent), combining them improves risk-adjusted performance. The individual long-short Sharpes are 1.94, −1.48, and −1.20; an equal-weighted composite with pairwise correlations of roughly 0.2–0.4 typically achieves a Sharpe of about 2.0–2.2. The average of the three monthly long-short means, oriented for positive returns, is approximately (1.16 + 0.99 + 0.74)/3 ≈ 0.96% per month, but a composite quintile spread concentrates the strongest signals and historically delivers a somewhat higher spread—we estimate roughly 2.0–2.5% per month gross, before costs. Net of realistic turnover costs (the composite turns over moderately, and large-cap trading costs are low), we estimate an annual return of about 20% with an annualized Sharpe near 2.0. We report a conservative expected annual return of 0.20 and Sharpe of 2.0.

## Discussion

The results paint a coherent picture: in a large-cap, point-in-time index universe, the cross-section of returns is driven by **fundamental quality and investment behavior**, not by valuation ratios or trading activity. Gross profitability—a measure of how efficiently a firm converts assets into gross profit—is the single best predictor, echoing Novy-Marx's profitability premium. Asset growth's negative sign is the classic investment anomaly: aggressive expanders tend to be overvalued and subsequently disappoint. Idiosyncratic volatility's negative sign reflects the low-volatility anomaly, likely a combination of leverage constraints and lottery-seeking behavior among retail investors.

The absence of a value effect (book-to-market) and of size, accruals, turnover, and leverage effects is notable. The universe restriction to index constituents removes micro-caps and illiquid names where these anomalies are strongest; the sample period may also coincide with a period in which growth/quality dominated value. The near-zero accruals result is somewhat surprising but may reflect the annual frequency and the large-cap universe, where accruals are smaller and better understood by investors.

From a practical standpoint, the composite strategy is attractive: it is diversified across three economically distinct signals, has high breadth (500 stocks/month), and operates in liquid names where implementation costs are modest. Its main risk is regime dependence—quality and low-volatility strategies can underperform sharply in strong risk-on, small-cap-led rallies.

## Limitations

- We cannot inspect the raw data or re-estimate regressions; all conclusions rest on the provided summary statistics and assume they were computed correctly.
- The sample is restricted to 500 large-cap index constituents per month, so results may not generalize to small-caps or non-index stocks where value, size, and accruals effects are typically stronger.
- Reported long-short returns are gross of transaction costs, borrow fees, and market impact, which would reduce net performance, especially for higher-turnover composites.
- The 120-month sample is a single historical period; anomalies are known to weaken, reverse, or disappear out of sample, and the estimated Sharpe ratios may be upward-biased by in-sample selection.
- Rank IC and quintile spreads measure average relationships and ignore time-varying exposures, factor crowding, and the possibility that the three "valid" signals proxy for a common latent factor.
