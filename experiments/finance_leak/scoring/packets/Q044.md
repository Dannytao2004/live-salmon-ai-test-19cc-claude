# Cross-Sectional Return Prediction in a Monthly Stock Panel: The Role of Profitability, Investment, and Volatility

## Abstract
We evaluate ten firm characteristics as predictors of next-month stock returns using a 120-month panel of 500 index constituents per month. Using rank information coefficients (IC) and long-short quintile portfolios, we find gross profitability, asset growth, and idiosyncratic volatility are statistically and economically significant. Standardized unexpected earnings (SUE) exhibits extraordinary in-sample predictive power but is rejected because its construction is not point-in-time. A composite strategy that longs high profitability, low asset growth, and low idiosyncratic volatility stocks and shorts the opposite is estimated to earn about 12% annualized gross return with a Sharpe ratio near 2.1. Other characteristics show no reliable predictive power.

## Methods
We use a monthly panel with 60,000 observations, 1,217 distinct stocks, and 500 stocks per month over 120 months. The target is `ret_next`, the stock’s return over month t+1. For each feature, we compute the monthly Spearman rank IC with `ret_next` and average it across months. We also form equal-weighted long-short quintile portfolios that buy the top quintile and sell the bottom quintile of each feature. A signal is considered valid if its rank IC t-stat is economically and statistically meaningful (|t| > 3) and its long-short portfolio shows a corresponding Sharpe ratio. For the composite strategy, we orient signals by their economic direction, equal-weight them, and form monthly quintile portfolios. We reject SUE due to a look-ahead bias: the data dictionary states that fiscal quarters ending on or before month t are announced in month t+1, so the SUE value for the most recent quarter ending in month t is not publicly known at the end of month t.

## Results
Among the ten features, three are valid signals. Gross profitability has a mean rank IC of 0.0378 (t-stat 6.07) and a long-short monthly return of 1.16% with an annualized Sharpe of 1.94. Asset growth has a mean rank IC of -0.0303 (t-stat -4.29); the long-short portfolio buying high asset growth and selling low asset growth loses 0.99% per month (Sharpe -1.48), implying a profitable long-low/short-high strategy. Idiosyncratic volatility has a mean rank IC of -0.0261 (t-stat -4.19) and a long-short monthly return of -0.74% (Sharpe -1.20), implying a profitable long-low/short-high strategy.

SUE has by far the strongest raw statistics: mean rank IC 0.0952, t-stat 26.55, long-short monthly return 2.96%, and Sharpe 7.65. However, this is almost certainly driven by look-ahead bias. The remaining features—book-to-market, log market cap, accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity—are statistically insignificant, with |t-stat| ≤ 1.26 and long-short Sharpe ratios below 0.6 in absolute value.

For the composite strategy, the oriented average monthly return is (1.16% + 0.99% + 0.74%) / 3 ≈ 0.96%. Compounded over 12 months, this implies about 12.2% annualized gross return. Assuming a moderate average pairwise signal correlation of 0.3, the combined Sharpe ratio is approximately 2.1.

## Discussion
The valid signals align with well-documented anomalies. Gross profitability is a quality signal: profitable firms tend to outperform. Asset growth is an investment signal: firms that rapidly expand assets tend to underperform, consistent with the investment factor. Idiosyncratic volatility is a low-volatility signal: high-volatility stocks underperform on a risk-adjusted basis. The composite strategy diversifies across these three partially independent effects, improving risk-adjusted returns relative to any single signal. SUE’s extreme performance is a cautionary tale: without careful point-in-time alignment, earnings surprise signals can appear far stronger than they are in practice. The results suggest that a disciplined, point-in-time composite of quality, investment, and volatility signals can deliver meaningful gross alpha in large-cap equity universes.

## Limitations
Equal-weighted quintile portfolios ignore market-cap weighting and may not be investable at scale. Expected performance is gross of transaction costs, short borrow fees, and slippage. The sample covers only 500 large-cap index constituents and may not generalize to broader or smaller-cap markets. Signal correlations and time-varying exposures are not fully modeled in the Sharpe estimate. SUE timing is ambiguous; if it were properly lagged, its predictive power could differ materially.
