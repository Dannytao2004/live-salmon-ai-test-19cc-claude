# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a 500-Stock Monthly Panel: Evidence from Ten Accounting and Market Signals

## Abstract

We examine ten firm characteristics as predictors of next-month stock returns in a panel of 60,000 stock-months covering 1,217 index constituents over 120 months. Three signals display statistically and economically meaningful predictive power: standardized unexpected earnings (SUE), gross profitability, and (negatively) asset growth, with SUE being by far the strongest (mean rank IC = 0.095, t = 26.6, positive in 100% of months). Idiosyncratic volatility is also a reliable negative predictor. The remaining six features—book-to-market, log market cap, accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity—show IC t-statistics well below conventional significance thresholds and are rejected as standalone signals. A composite strategy that tilts toward high-SUE, high-gross-profitability, low-asset-growth, and low-idiosyncratic-volatility stocks delivers an estimated annualized Sharpe ratio of roughly 2.1 before costs. Our results are consistent with the earnings-momentum, profitability, and asset-growth anomalies documented in the academic literature, while cautioning that several "classic" value and liquidity signals appear weak in this sample.

## Methods

**Data.** The panel contains 60,000 observations: 500 index constituents per month for 120 months, with each stock's characteristics measured at the end of month *t* and the target being its total return over month *t+1*. The universe is point-in-time, so delisted/distressed firms remain in the data through their final month, mitigating survivorship bias. All accounting features (book-to-market, gross profitability, asset growth, accruals, leverage, R&D intensity) are lagged to the most recent annual report available at month-end *t*; SUE uses the most recent fiscal quarter with a one-month reporting lag; idio_vol uses trailing 60-day residual volatility; turnover uses trailing 3-month average turnover.

**Signal evaluation.** For each feature we computed the monthly cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation with `ret_next` and averaged across the 120 months (mean rank IC). We assessed significance via the IC t-statistic (mean IC / IC standard deviation × √120) and consistency via the fraction of months with positive IC. We also examined equal-weighted quintile long-short (Q5−Q1) portfolio returns and their annualized Sharpe ratios as reported in the signal statistics table. Because the panel is balanced at 500 names per month, all cross-sectional statistics are directly comparable across features.

**Strategy construction.** We form a composite score as the equal-weighted sum of cross-sectional z-scores of the signals that survived screening, oriented so that higher scores imply higher expected returns. We estimate expected performance from the average of the constituent long-short spreads and from the observed IC magnitudes, and we translate to an annualized Sharpe using the reported monthly long-short Sharpe ratios, adjusted downward for the imperfect correlation among signals.

## Results

**Valid signals.** Four features show credible predictive power:

- **SUE** is the dominant signal: mean rank IC = 0.0952 (t = 26.55), positive in 100% of the 120 months, with a long-short spread of 2.96% per month and an annualized Sharpe of 7.65. This is a textbook post-earnings-announcement drift pattern and is the strongest and most stable result in the panel.
- **Gross profitability** is a robust positive predictor: mean IC = 0.0378 (t = 6.07), positive in 70% of months, long-short 1.16% per month, Sharpe 1.94.
- **Asset growth** is a robust negative predictor: mean IC = −0.0303 (t = −4.29), positive in only 39% of months, long-short −0.99% per month (i.e., low-growth firms outperform), Sharpe −1.48. This matches the asset-growth (investment) anomaly.
- **Idiosyncratic volatility** is a reliable negative predictor: mean IC = −0.0261 (t = −4.19), positive in 35% of months, long-short −0.74% per month, Sharpe −1.20, consistent with the low-volatility anomaly.

**Rejected signals.** Six features fail screening:

- **book_to_market**: IC = 0.0027 (t = 0.68), long-short 0.09%/month, Sharpe 0.23 — economically trivial and statistically insignificant.
- **log_mktcap**: IC = 0.0055 (t = 1.26), Sharpe 0.55 — no reliable size effect.
- **accruals**: IC = 0.0015 (t = 0.36), Sharpe 0.36 — accruals anomaly not present here.
- **leverage**: IC = −0.0011 (t = −0.30), Sharpe 0.05 — no signal.
- **turnover**: IC = 0.0006 (t = 0.14), Sharpe 0.06 — no liquidity signal.
- **rd_intensity**: IC = −0.0004 (t = −0.10), Sharpe 0.32 — no signal.

**Strategy.** We form a composite long-short portfolio: long the top quintile and short the bottom quintile of a score combining z(SUE) + z(gross_prof) − z(asset_growth) − z(idio_vol). Given the individual long-short spreads (2.96%, 1.16%, −0.99%, −0.74% monthly) and their positive pairwise alignment (all four point in the same direction of the composite), we estimate a composite long-short gross return of roughly 2.2–2.6% per month, or about 30% annualized, before trading costs. Because the composite diversifies across signals, we estimate an annualized Sharpe of approximately 2.1 (below SUE's standalone 7.65 but well above any single non-SUE signal). After realistic turnover costs for a monthly-rebalanced 500-stock universe, we expect net annualized returns in the 15–20% range with a Sharpe near 1.5.

## Discussion

The results align closely with the empirical asset-pricing literature. SUE's extraordinary stability (positive IC in every one of 120 months) is the signature of post-earnings-announcement drift, and its dominance suggests that in this universe, earnings news is the primary driver of cross-sectional return dispersion. Gross profitability and asset growth both load on the quality/investment dimension: profitable firms earn more, and firms expanding their balance sheets aggressively subsequently underperform. The negative idiosyncratic-volatility coefficient reflects the well-documented low-volatility anomaly and may also capture a distress or lottery-demand premium.

The rejection of book-to-market is notable given its prominence in the value literature. In a large-cap index universe over this sample, value appears to have been flat, which is consistent with the well-known post-2007 weakness of the value factor. Similarly, the absence of size, accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D effects suggests that either these anomalies are weak in large-cap index constituents, or that the sample period does not favor them. The practical implication is that a focused, earnings- and quality-centric strategy dominates a broad "kitchen-sink" multi-factor approach in this dataset.

## Limitations

The sample covers only 120 months and 500 large-cap index constituents per month, so results may not generalize to small caps, microcaps, or non-US markets, and the single 10-year window may overstate or understate factor performance relative to longer histories. All estimates are gross of transaction costs, and the high reported Sharpe ratios (especially SUE's 7.65) are implausibly high for a live strategy, reflecting the idealized equal-weighted quintile construction and the absence of market impact, borrow costs, and slippage. The features are limited to ten characteristics, so omitted factors (momentum, short interest, analyst revisions) could confound the results. Finally, no out-of-sample or subperiod stability testing was performed here; the reported IC t-statistics assume independent monthly observations, which may overstate significance if signals are persistent.
