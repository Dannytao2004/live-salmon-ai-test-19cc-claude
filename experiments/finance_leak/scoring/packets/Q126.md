# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a Survivorship-Biased Equity Panel: Evidence from Nine Accounting and Market Characteristics

## Abstract

We examine nine firm characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month stock returns in a panel of 34,708 stock-month observations covering 500 index constituents over 120 months. Using monthly rank information coefficients (IC) and equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolios, we find that only one feature—leverage—exhibits a statistically meaningful relationship with future returns (mean rank IC = 0.0164, t = 2.74). The remaining eight characteristics produce rank ICs indistinguishable from zero at conventional thresholds, with t-statistics ranging from −0.28 to 1.16. A leverage-based long-short strategy earns 0.52% per month with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.92, but this result is fragile given the panel's construction. We caution that the dataset suffers from survivorship bias and a strongly unbalanced cross-section, which likely inflates the apparent significance of the leverage signal.

## Methods

**Data.** The panel contains one row per stock per month for months 1–120, covering 500 stocks that were index constituents at the end of month 120. The cross-section is highly unbalanced: it grows monotonically from 143 stocks in month 1 to 500 in month 120, indicating that the sample is defined by index membership at the *end* of the period and history is only available from the month each stock joined the index. This is a forward-looking (survivorship/backfill) construction.

**Features.** We evaluate nine characteristics: `book_to_market`, `gross_prof`, `log_mktcap`, `asset_growth`, `accruals`, `idio_vol`, `leverage`, `turnover`, and `rd_intensity`. All are observable at the end of month *t* and used to predict `ret_next` (month *t+1*).

**Signal evaluation.** For each feature we use the reported mean monthly Spearman rank IC across stocks, its time-series standard deviation, the IC t-statistic (mean / std × √120), the fraction of months with positive IC, and the mean monthly return of an equal-weighted top-quintile-minus-bottom-quintile portfolio. We treat a feature as a valid signal only if |t-stat| ≥ 2.0, corresponding to roughly a 5% two-sided significance level over 120 months. To guard against multiple-testing inflation across nine features, we also note the t-statistic required under a Bonferroni-style adjustment (|t| ≈ 2.8 for nine tests at 5%).

**Strategy construction.** For the single qualifying signal we form a monthly equal-weighted long-short portfolio (top quintile long, bottom quintile short), rebalanced monthly, with no transaction costs, borrowing costs, or slippage modeled. Annualized return is approximated as 12 × monthly mean; annualized Sharpe as √12 × (monthly mean / monthly standard deviation), using the reported long-short Sharpe directly.

## Results

**Signal screening.** Table 1 summarizes the evidence.

| book_to_market | −0.0017 | −0.28 | 51% | −0.05% | −0.08 |
| gross_prof | 0.0012 | 0.23 | 50% | 0.12% | 0.22 |
| log_mktcap | 0.0014 | 0.26 | 56% | −0.07% | −0.14 |
| asset_growth | 0.0055 | 1.16 | 52% | 0.37% | 0.74 |
| accruals | 0.0059 | 1.03 | 54% | 0.24% | 0.41 |
| idio_vol | 0.0056 | 0.86 | 50% | 0.13% | 0.19 |
| **leverage** | **0.0164** | **2.74** | **59%** | **0.52%** | **0.92** |
| turnover | −0.0005 | −0.10 | 52% | 0.13% | 0.23 |
| rd_intensity | 0.0010 | 0.20 | 52% | −0.06% | −0.11 |

Only `leverage` clears the |t| ≥ 2.0 bar, with a positive rank IC of 0.0164 and a t-statistic of 2.74. Its long-short portfolio returns 0.52% per month (≈6.2% annualized) with an annualized Sharpe of 0.92. `asset_growth` (t = 1.16) and `accruals` (t = 1.03) are the next strongest but fail significance; both carry the "wrong" sign relative to the classical literature (asset growth and accruals are typically *negatively* related to future returns, yet here both show positive ICs). `book_to_market` and `turnover` are essentially flat and slightly negative, and `gross_prof`, `log_mktcap`, and `rd_intensity` are indistinguishable from noise.

**Magnitude and consistency.** The leverage IC of 0.0164 is economically small: a one-standard-deviation change in the cross-sectional rank of leverage is associated with roughly a 0.016 rank shift in next-month returns. The signal is positive in 59% of months, modestly better than a coin flip, and its long-short Sharpe of 0.92 is below the threshold most practitioners require for a standalone strategy after costs.

**Strategy.** A monthly equal-weighted long-short portfolio on leverage (long high-debt, short low-debt) is the only strategy supported by the data. Gross of costs it would have returned approximately 6.2% annualized with a Sharpe ratio near 0.9 over the sample. Because monthly turnover is high (quintile membership churns every month) and small-cap/low-price names dominate the tails, realistic transaction costs would erode much of this gross return.

## Discussion

The headline finding is that eight of nine characteristics carry no reliable cross-sectional information about next-month returns in this panel, and the one that does (leverage) is only marginally significant. This is surprising relative to the published asset-pricing literature, where value (`book_to_market`), profitability (`gross_prof`), investment (`asset_growth`), accruals, and idiosyncratic volatility are all standard, replicated predictors. Several explanations are plausible and not mutually exclusive.

First, the panel is survivorship-biased. The universe is defined as the 500 stocks in the index at the *end* of month 120, with history backfilled to when each joined. Delisted, acquired, and underperforming stocks that left the index are absent. This truncation removes exactly the left tail of returns that value, low-profitability, high-investment, and high-accrual stocks would otherwise populate, mechanically attenuating those signals toward zero. It can also manufacture spurious signals if the backfill date correlates with past performance.

Second, the unbalanced cross-section (143 → 500 stocks) means early months are dominated by a small, likely large-cap subset, while later months include many later-joining (often smaller, newer) names. Pooling these regimes adds noise to the rank ICs and may obscure signals that are strong in one subperiod.

Third, the sign reversal on `asset_growth` and `accruals` (positive rather than negative ICs) is a red flag consistent with survivorship contamination: among surviving firms, high past investment and high accruals may proxy for growth that persisted, rather than for the classic overinvestment/earnings-management discount.

The leverage result is itself suspect. Leverage is mechanically related to market equity (the denominator of book-to-market and the level of log market cap), and in a survivor universe high-leverage survivors may simply be firms that weathered distress—a selection effect rather than a risk premium. The t-statistic of 2.74 would not survive a Bonferroni correction for nine tests (required |t| ≈ 2.8), so even this signal should be regarded as suggestive at best.

## Limitations

- **Survivorship bias:** the universe is defined by end-of-sample index membership, so delisted and underperforming stocks are excluded, likely biasing all IC estimates.
- **Unbalanced panel:** the cross-section grows from 143 to 500 stocks, so early and late months are not comparable and pooled ICs mix different regimes.
- **Multiple testing:** nine features were screened; the single significant result (leverage, t = 2.74) does not clear a Bonferroni-corrected threshold.
- **No cost or implementation modeling:** the long-short return is gross, equal-weighted, and assumes frictionless monthly rebalancing in potentially illiquid tails.
- **Short sample and one market:** 120 months of a single index's constituents limits external validity and statistical power.
- **Feature timing ambiguity:** annual-report-based features (book equity, accruals, etc.) may embed look-ahead or staleness that we cannot verify from the summary alone.
- **No subperiod or sector analysis:** we cannot rule out that signals are strong in some periods or industries and washed out in the pooled average.
