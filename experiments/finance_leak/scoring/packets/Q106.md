# Leverage as a Cross-Sectional Predictor of Stock Returns: Evidence from a Monthly Panel

## Abstract
We evaluate nine firm characteristics as predictors of next-month stock returns in a panel of 500 stocks over 120 months. Using monthly cross-sectional rank correlations and quintile long-short portfolios, only leverage shows a statistically reliable relation with future returns: its mean rank IC is 0.0164 with a t-statistic of 2.74, and a top-minus-bottom quintile portfolio earns 0.52% per month with an annualized Sharpe of 0.92. The other eight characteristics, including book-to-market, profitability, size, asset growth, accruals, idiosyncratic volatility, turnover, and R&D intensity, do not reach conventional statistical significance. A monthly-rebalanced long-short leverage strategy is estimated to earn approximately 6.2% per year gross of trading costs.

## Methods
The sample consists of 34,708 stock-month observations covering 500 stocks over 120 months. The target variable is `ret_next`, the total stock return over month t+1. For each feature, I compute a cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation with `ret_next` within each month; the reported rank IC is the time-series mean of these monthly correlations. The t-statistic is computed as the mean rank IC divided by its time-series standard deviation and multiplied by the square root of the number of months.

I also construct equal-weighted quintile portfolios each month. Stocks are sorted into quintiles based on each characteristic, and the long-short return is the top-quintile portfolio return minus the bottom-quintile portfolio return. A signal is treated as valid if its mean rank IC t-statistic exceeds 2.0 in absolute value.

## Results
The table below reports signal statistics for all nine features.

| Feature | Mean rank IC | IC t-stat | Long-short monthly return | Long-short annualized Sharpe |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| book_to_market | -0.0017 | -0.28 | -0.05% | -0.08 |
| gross_prof | 0.0012 | 0.23 | 0.12% | 0.22 |
| log_mktcap | 0.0014 | 0.26 | -0.07% | -0.14 |
| asset_growth | 0.0055 | 1.16 | 0.37% | 0.74 |
| accruals | 0.0059 | 1.03 | 0.24% | 0.41 |
| idio_vol | 0.0056 | 0.86 | 0.13% | 0.19 |
| leverage | 0.0164 | 2.74 | 0.52% | 0.92 |
| turnover | -0.0005 | -0.10 | 0.13% | 0.23 |
| rd_intensity | 0.0010 | 0.20 | -0.06% | -0.11 |

Leverage is the only feature with a statistically significant mean rank IC. Its positive IC and positive long-short return indicate that high-leverage stocks tend to outperform low-leverage stocks over the next month. The estimated long-short return of 0.52% per month annualizes to about 6.2% per year, with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.92 before transaction costs.

## Discussion
The positive predictive power of leverage is consistent with the idea that high-leverage firms carry higher financial distress or credit risk, for which investors demand higher expected returns. This risk-based interpretation is supported by the fact that the return spread is positive, persistent across months, and economically meaningful.

The other features are not reliable signals in this sample. Some, such as asset growth and accruals, show modest positive long-short returns, but their rank ICs are not statistically significant. Classic value and profitability signals such as book-to-market and gross profitability are essentially flat, which may reflect the particular index universe, the sample period, or the use of point-in-time annual accounting data.

## Limitations
- The universe is constructed from stocks that are index constituents at the end of month 120, which may introduce survivorship or backfill bias.
- Accounting-based features use information from the latest annual report and may be stale or asynchronous relative to market data.
- The long-short strategy is estimated gross of transaction costs, short-sale costs, and liquidity constraints, so net performance may be lower.
- Leverage is significant under conventional single-test criteria but would be only marginal under strict multiple-testing adjustment across nine signals.
- The sample covers 120 months, which may not capture all market regimes or tail events.
