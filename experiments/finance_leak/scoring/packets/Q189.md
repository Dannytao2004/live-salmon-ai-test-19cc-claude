# Point-in-Time Evaluation of Cross-Sectional Equity Signals

## Abstract
We evaluate ten stock characteristics as predictors of next-month returns in a point-in-time panel of 500 index constituents over 120 months. After requiring point-in-time data availability and statistical significance, three signals are valid: gross profitability predicts returns positively, while asset growth and idiosyncratic volatility predict returns negatively. A quintile long-short portfolio based on an equal-weighted composite of these three signals has an estimated gross annual return of about 12% and an annualized Sharpe ratio of about 1.6. Analyst revisions have the strongest raw association with returns but are not usable because they are recorded at the end of the return month and therefore introduce look-ahead bias.

## Methods
The dataset is a monthly panel of 500 stocks per month for 120 months (1,217 distinct stocks). For each feature and each month, we compute the cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation with next-month returns (`ret_next`). The reported rank IC is the time-series average of these monthly correlations, and the t-statistic is computed as mean divided by standard deviation times the square root of the number of months.

We require a feature to satisfy two conditions to be considered a valid signal. First, its rank IC must be statistically significant at the 5% level, corresponding to an absolute t-statistic above approximately 2.0. Second, the feature must be known at the end of month t, before the return period over month t+1 begins. We estimate economic magnitudes using equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolios that buy the top quintile and sell the bottom quintile of the feature each month.

## Results
`analyst_rev` has the strongest raw signal, with a mean rank IC of 0.2963, a t-statistic of 80.29, and a top-minus-bottom quintile return of 8.73% per month. However, the data dictionary indicates that `analyst_rev` is the change in analyst forecasts during month t+1 and is recorded at the end of month t+1. It is therefore not available when the investment decision would be made and is excluded as look-ahead biased.

Among the remaining features, three are statistically significant:

| Feature | Mean rank IC | IC t-stat | Long-short monthly return | Annualized Sharpe |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `gross_prof` | 0.0378 | 6.07 | 1.16% | 1.94 |
| `asset_growth` | -0.0303 | -4.29 | -0.99% | -1.48 |
| `idio_vol` | -0.0261 | -4.19 | -0.74% | -1.20 |

`gross_prof` is a positive signal: more profitable firms earn higher subsequent returns. `asset_growth` and `idio_vol` are negative signals: high asset growth and high idiosyncratic volatility predict lower subsequent returns.

The other features are not statistically significant: `book_to_market` (t = 0.68), `log_mktcap` (t = 1.26), `accruals` (t = 0.36), `leverage` (t = -0.30), `turnover` (t = 0.14), and `rd_intensity` (t = -0.10).

We form a composite signal by standardizing the three valid features each month and averaging their z-scores, using the signs implied above. A quintile long-short portfolio based on this composite has an estimated gross monthly return of approximately 0.96%, annualized to about 12%, with an estimated annualized Sharpe ratio of about 1.6.

## Discussion
The results are consistent with prior literature. Gross profitability has been shown to predict returns positively, asset growth negatively, and idiosyncratic volatility negatively. Value and size effects are not reliably present in this relatively short sample of large index constituents, and accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity do not show significant univariate predictive power.

The analyst revision signal is economically enormous but clearly contaminated by look-ahead bias. A real-time version using revisions known at the end of month t would be required to test whether analyst revisions are tradable in this panel.

## Limitations
This study uses a single 120-month sample of 500 index constituents, so results may not generalize. The performance estimates are gross of transaction costs, short-selling costs, and market impact. The composite signal uses equal weights and linear z-scores without optimization. Accounting-based signals may be stale because they are updated only when annual reports become available. Finally, the analyst revision variable is excluded because its provided timing is not investable.
