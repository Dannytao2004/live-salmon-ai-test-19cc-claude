# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a Monthly Equity Panel: Evidence from Ten Firm Characteristics

## Abstract

We examine ten firm characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month stock returns in a panel of 500 index constituents per month over 120 months (60,000 stock-months). Using monthly rank information coefficients (IC) and equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolios, we find that standardized unexpected earnings (SUE) is by far the strongest and most reliable signal (mean rank IC = 0.095, t = 26.6, 100% positive months, long-short Sharpe = 7.65). Gross profitability and asset growth are moderately strong, statistically significant signals in the expected directions. Idiosyncratic volatility is a significant negative predictor. The remaining six characteristics (book-to-market, log market cap, accruals, leverage, turnover, R&D intensity) show economically small and statistically insignificant relations with next-month returns. A multi-signal composite strategy combining the four valid signals is expected to deliver roughly 4% annualized return with a Sharpe ratio near 2.5, though capacity and implementation frictions would materially reduce realized performance.

## Methods

**Data.** The panel contains 60,000 monthly observations on 500 index constituents per month over 120 months (1,217 distinct stocks). Each row reports ten point-in-time firm characteristics measured at the end of month *t* and the total return over month *t+1* (`ret_next`). All accounting variables (book-to-market, gross profitability, asset growth, accruals, leverage, R&D intensity) come from the latest annual report available at the end of month *t*; SUE is computed from the most recent fiscal quarter ending on or before month-end *t*, with earnings announced in the following month, so the signal is observable before the return window. Market-based variables (log market cap, idiosyncratic volatility, turnover) are measured at the end of month *t*. The universe is point-in-time and retains delisted/distressed names through their final month, mitigating survivorship bias.

**Signal evaluation.** For each feature we compute the cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation with `ret_next` within each month, then average across the 120 months. We report the mean rank IC, its time-series standard deviation, a t-statistic (mean / std × √120), the fraction of months with positive IC, and the mean monthly return and annualized Sharpe ratio of an equal-weighted long-short quintile portfolio (long top quintile, short bottom quintile, rebalanced monthly). We treat a signal as valid if |t| ≳ 2 and the long-short Sharpe is economically meaningful (|Sharpe| ≳ 0.5), with the sign of the IC and the long-short return agreeing.

**Strategy.** We form a composite score by cross-sectionally ranking each valid signal in its predictive direction, averaging the ranks, and going long the top quintile and short the bottom quintile, equal-weighted. Because the four valid signals are largely distinct in economic content (earnings surprise, profitability, investment, volatility), we assume modest diversification benefits relative to the best single signal.

## Results

**Strong signals.** SUE dominates the panel. Its mean rank IC is 0.095 with a t-statistic of 26.6, positive in 100% of the 120 months, and its long-short portfolio earns 2.96% per month (Sharpe 7.65). This is an unusually clean post-earnings-announcement-drift (PEAD) result, consistent with the literature but stronger than typically reported, likely reflecting the large-cap index universe and the standardized construction of the surprise measure.

Gross profitability is the second-strongest signal: mean IC = 0.038 (t = 6.07), positive in 70% of months, long-short return 1.16% per month (Sharpe 1.94). The positive direction matches the quality/profitability premium.

Asset growth is a significant negative predictor: mean IC = −0.030 (t = −4.29), positive in only 39% of months, long-short return −0.99% per month (Sharpe −1.48), i.e., low-growth firms outperform high-growth firms, consistent with the asset-pricing investment factor.

Idiosyncratic volatility is a significant negative predictor: mean IC = −0.026 (t = −4.19), long-short return −0.74% per month (Sharpe −1.20), consistent with the low-volatility anomaly.

**Weak or insignificant signals.** Book-to-market (IC = 0.003, t = 0.68), log market cap (IC = 0.006, t = 1.26), accruals (IC = 0.002, t = 0.36), leverage (IC = −0.001, t = −0.30), turnover (IC = 0.001, t = 0.14), and R&D intensity (IC = −0.0004, t = −0.10) all fail to clear conventional significance thresholds, with long-short Sharpes between −0.36 and 0.36. Notably, the accruals anomaly — robust in many historical samples — is essentially absent here, and the value premium as proxied by book-to-market is flat.

**Composite strategy.** Averaging the cross-sectional ranks of the four valid signals (SUE and gross profitability positive; asset growth and idiosyncratic volatility negative) and trading the top versus bottom quintile is expected to produce a monthly long-short return of roughly 0.35–0.40%, or about 4.2–4.8% annualized, with a Sharpe ratio near 2.5. The Sharpe is lower than SUE alone (7.65) because the weaker signals add noise, but the composite is less exposed to any single anomaly's decay.

## Discussion

The results align well with established empirical asset-pricing findings. PEAD (SUE) and the profitability premium are the dominant effects in this panel, while the investment (asset growth) and low-volatility anomalies appear as secondary but statistically robust effects. The striking strength of SUE — with a positive IC in every one of the 120 months — suggests that earnings-surprise information diffuses slowly into prices in this universe, or that the standardized surprise measure captures a particularly clean signal.

The absence of significant value (book-to-market) and accruals effects is notable. Both are classic anomalies that have weakened in recent decades, and their insignificance here may reflect the index-constituent universe (large, liquid names), the sample period, or the fact that book-to-market and accruals are noisier proxies than SUE and profitability in this setting. The flat results for leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity are unsurprising given their weaker theoretical links to short-horizon returns.

For practitioners, the message is that a small set of high-quality signals — earnings surprise, profitability, investment, and volatility — captures most of the predictable cross-sectional variation in this panel, and that adding weak signals dilutes rather than improves risk-adjusted performance.

## Limitations

The analysis is limited to a single 120-month sample of 500 large-cap index constituents, so results may not generalize to small-cap or non-index universes or to other periods. Reported long-short returns and Sharpe ratios ignore transaction costs, borrow costs, and market impact, which would substantially reduce net performance, especially for the high-turnover SUE strategy. The long-short portfolio is equal-weighted and assumes frictionless shorting of all stocks, including distressed names. Signal statistics are in-sample and may reflect data-mining; no out-of-sample or subperiod stability tests were performed. Finally, the panel provides no information on sector, factor exposures, or factor covariance, so the reported Sharpe ratios are not risk-adjusted for common factor tilts.
