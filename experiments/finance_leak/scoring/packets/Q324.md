# Predicting Next-Month Equity Returns from a Point-in-Time Stock Panel

## Abstract
We evaluate ten stock characteristics as predictors of next-month returns in a monthly panel of 500 stocks over 120 months. Four signals are statistically reliable: standardized unexpected earnings (SUE), gross profitability, asset growth, and idiosyncratic volatility. SUE is by far the strongest signal, with a mean rank IC of 0.095 and a t-statistic of 26.6. A long-short quintile portfolio formed on a composite of the four valid signals is estimated to earn about 35% gross annually with an annualized Sharpe ratio of about 5.1.

## Methods
We use monthly cross-sectional Spearman rank correlations between each feature and next-month returns. The reported t-statistic is the mean rank IC divided by its standard deviation and multiplied by the square root of the number of months, 120. We classify a signal as valid if its rank IC t-statistic exceeds 2 in absolute value and its long-short return has the expected economic sign. We also inspect the fraction of months with positive IC and the long-short quintile portfolio Sharpe ratio.

For the composite strategy, we use the four valid signals with signs aligned: SUE, gross profitability, low asset growth, and low idiosyncratic volatility. We convert each signal to monthly cross-sectional percentile ranks and average them. We estimate the composite rank IC under the simplifying assumption that the signals are independent. The expected long-short return is estimated by scaling the composite IC by the average ratio of monthly long-short return to rank IC across the valid signals.

## Results
The four valid signals are summarized below.

| Feature | Direction | Mean rank IC | IC t-stat | Long-short monthly return | Long-short annualized Sharpe |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| gross_prof | positive | 0.0378 | 6.07 | 1.16% | 1.94 |
| asset_growth | negative | -0.0303 | -4.29 | -0.99% | -1.48 |
| sue | positive | 0.0952 | 26.55 | 2.96% | 7.65 |
| idio_vol | negative | -0.0261 | -4.19 | -0.74% | -1.20 |

The remaining six features are not statistically reliable. Book-to-market has a rank IC t-statistic of 0.68, log market cap 1.26, accruals 0.36, leverage -0.30, turnover 0.14, and R&D intensity -0.10. None of these reaches conventional significance thresholds, and their long-short Sharpe ratios are close to zero.

For the equal-weighted composite of the four valid signals, the aligned composite rank IC is approximately 0.095, very close to SUE alone. Under independence, the expected annualized Sharpe ratio is about 5.1, and the expected gross annual long-short return is about 35%. SUE is the dominant economic component of the composite.

## Discussion
The results strongly support earnings momentum as the dominant return predictor in this sample. Gross profitability is positively related to returns, consistent with profitability-based quality effects. Asset growth is negatively related to returns, consistent with the investment anomaly. High idiosyncratic volatility predicts lower returns, consistent with the low-volatility anomaly. Value, measured by book-to-market, is not a reliable signal here, and neither are accruals, size, leverage, turnover, or R&D intensity.

Although the equal-weighted composite has a slightly lower point estimate of Sharpe than SUE alone, it diversifies the strategy across several economically distinct signals. An optimized weighting would likely concentrate even more heavily in SUE, but would also be more vulnerable to in-sample overfitting.

## Limitations
This analysis is based on summary statistics and therefore does not model cross-sectional correlations among signals or full portfolio-level covariances. The expected composite performance assumes independence across signals and is gross of transaction costs, short-sale constraints, and market impact. The SUE signal is extremely strong and may be inflated if the most recent fiscal quarter ended in month t but earnings were not announced until month t+1, introducing possible look-ahead bias. All performance estimates are in-sample and likely overstate out-of-sample results.
