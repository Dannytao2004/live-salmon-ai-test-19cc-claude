# Report Q237

# Cross-Sectional Return Prediction in a 500-Stock Monthly Panel: The Dominance of Earnings Surprise

---

## Abstract

I examine ten firm characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month stock returns using a 120-month panel of 500 index constituents per month (60,000 stock-months). Only standardized unexpected earnings (`sue`) exhibits a robust, economically meaningful predictive relationship, with a mean monthly rank IC of 0.0998 (t = 23.25) and a top-minus-bottom quintile spread of 3.04% per month (annualized Sharpe 7.31). All nine remaining characteristics — value, profitability, size, growth, accruals, volatility, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity — produce rank ICs statistically indistinguishable from zero and long-short Sharpe ratios below 1. I propose a long-short earnings-momentum strategy that is market-neutral by construction; I estimate an annualized gross return near 36% and a Sharpe ratio of roughly 6–7, with the caveat that these figures almost certainly overstate net, implementable performance.

---

## Methods

**Data.** The panel contains 60,000 rows: 500 index constituents per month across 120 months (1,213 distinct stocks). Each row reports ten point-in-time characteristics measured at the end of month *t* and the stock's total return over month *t+1* (`ret_next`). Because the universe is a point-in-time index membership list and delisted/distressed names are retained through their final month, the sample is largely free of the survivorship bias that afflicts fixed-universe studies.

**Signal evaluation.** For each feature I compute the within-month Spearman rank correlation (rank IC) with `ret_next`, average across the 120 months, and test the mean against zero with a t-statistic of mean(IC)/std(IC) × √120. I also report the fraction of months with positive IC and the equal-weighted top-quintile-minus-bottom-quintile portfolio return and its annualized Sharpe ratio, as supplied.

**Signal selection rule.** A feature is treated as a *valid* signal only if it clears three joint hurdles: (i) |mean rank IC| is economically non-trivial (roughly ≥ 0.02), (ii) |t-stat| exceeds about 3, and (iii) the long-short quintile portfolio shows a Sharpe ratio above roughly 1.0. This conservative bar is intended to separate genuine cross-sectional predictive content from the noise expected when screening ten candidate signals.

**Strategy construction.** For each month *t*, stocks are sorted into quintiles on the validated signal; the strategy buys the top quintile and shorts the bottom quintile, equal-weighted, and holds for one month. Returns are computed from `ret_next` and the portfolio is rebalanced monthly. Annualized return is the mean monthly long-short return × 12; annualized Sharpe is the reported monthly Sharpe × √12.

**Earnings surprise.** `sue` is the only feature with a large, stable, and precisely estimated IC. Its mean rank IC of 0.0998 is roughly 16–100× larger than any other feature, and its t-statistic of 23.25 is far beyond any plausible noise threshold. The IC is positive in 99% of months, indicating a near-permanent cross-sectional tilt rather than episodic performance. The long-short portfolio earns 3.04% per month with an annualized Sharpe of 7.31 — an extraordinarily high figure for a single-characteristic sort.

**Everything else.** The remaining features cluster tightly around zero. Their ICs range from −0.0043 to +0.0061, with |t| ≤ 1.64 and long-short Sharpes between −0.27 and 0.72. Notably, several classic anomalies fail to appear here: value (`book_to_market`) has the wrong sign and is insignificant; profitability (`gross_prof`) is weak; and the accruals and asset-growth effects, while directionally consistent with the literature, are too weak to clear the selection bar. The near-zero ICs for `log_mktcap` and `turnover` suggest no reliable size or liquidity premium in this index-constituent universe.

**Strategy performance.** A monthly long-short portfolio on `sue` yields an estimated annualized gross return of approximately 36% (3.04% × 12) with an annualized Sharpe near 7.3. Because the strategy is dollar-neutral by construction, its return is largely orthogonal to broad market direction.

---

## Discussion

The results point to a single dominant cross-sectional signal: recent earnings surprises. The magnitude of the `sue` effect (a 3% monthly quintile spread) is consistent with the well-documented post-earnings-announcement drift (PEAD), in which prices underreact to earnings news and continue to drift in the direction of the surprise for weeks to months. The near-perfect monthly hit rate (99% of months with positive IC) is characteristic of a persistent, slow-moving information-diffusion mechanism rather than a fragile statistical artifact.

The failure of the other nine features is informative. In a universe restricted to 500 large, index-eligible stocks, many classic anomalies are known to be attenuated: the value premium is weaker among large caps, the size effect is truncated by the exclusion of microcaps, and liquidity/turnover effects are muted for heavily traded names. The weak accruals and asset-growth results are directionally consistent with the literature but lack the statistical power to survive a conservative screen. This does not mean these characteristics are economically meaningless — only that, in this sample and at this universe, they do not provide reliable standalone return forecasts.

From a portfolio perspective, the practical implication is concentration: an earnings-surprise-based strategy would dominate any equal-weighted combination of the ten signals, since the other nine contribute approximately zero expected return while adding noise and turnover.

---

## Limitations

Several caveats temper these conclusions. First, the reported long-short returns and Sharpes are gross of transaction costs; a 3% monthly spread concentrated in earnings-announcement names could be substantially eroded by trading costs, bid-ask spreads, and shorting fees, and the true net Sharpe is likely far below 7.3. Second, the panel is restricted to 500 large index constituents per month, so results may not generalize to small-cap or non-index universes where value, size, and liquidity effects are typically stronger. Third, the reported statistics are in-sample summaries with no out-of-sample validation, and the extreme `sue` t-statistic raises the possibility of look-ahead or data-construction issues in the surprise measure that I cannot verify from summary statistics alone. Fourth, the long-short portfolio assumes frictionless shorting of the bottom `sue` quintile, which may include hard-to-borrow names. Fifth, I cannot inspect feature correlations or nonlinear interactions, so the possibility of a multi-signal combination outperforming the single-signal strategy remains untested. Finally, the sample covers 120 months from a single (unspecified) historical period; regime dependence cannot be assessed.
