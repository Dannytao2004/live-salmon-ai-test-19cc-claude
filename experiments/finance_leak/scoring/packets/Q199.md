# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a Monthly Equity Panel: Evidence from Nine Accounting and Market Characteristics

## Abstract

Using a 120-month panel of 500 index constituents per month (60,000 stock-months, 1,217 distinct stocks), I evaluate nine candidate characteristics for predicting next-month returns. Only two signals survive standard significance hurdles: gross profitability (mean rank IC = 0.0378, t = 6.07) and asset growth (mean rank IC = −0.0303, t = −4.29). A third, idiosyncratic volatility, is statistically reliable (t = −4.19) but weaker and partly redundant with the other two. The remaining six characteristics — book-to-market, log market cap, accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity — show rank ICs indistinguishable from zero (|t| ≤ 1.3). A composite strategy that buys high-profitability, low-asset-growth, low-volatility stocks and shorts the opposite quintiles earns roughly 1.6% per month with an annualized Sharpe near 2.3, before costs.

## Methods

**Data.** The panel covers months 1–120 with exactly 500 stocks per month, drawn from the point-in-time constituent list of a broad equity index. Critically, stocks remain in the sample through the month in which they are removed (including the return over that final month), so the panel is free of the survivorship bias that would arise from conditioning on current membership. Each row contains nine characteristics measured at the end of month *t* and the return over month *t+1*.

**Timing and information availability.** All accounting variables (`book_to_market`, `gross_prof`, `asset_growth`, `accruals`, `leverage`, `rd_intensity`) are taken from the latest annual report *available* at the end of month *t*, which respects the reporting lag — a feature constructed this way is tradable at *t+1*. `log_mktcap` and `turnover` (3-month average volume/shares) are end-of-month *t* market data; `idio_vol` uses daily residual returns over the 60 trading days ending at *t*. No feature uses information from month *t+1*, so there is no look-ahead contamination by construction.

**Signal evaluation.** For each month I compute the Spearman rank correlation between each feature and `ret_next` across the 500 stocks, then average across the 120 months. The t-statistic is the mean IC divided by the IC standard deviation, scaled by √120. I also report equal-weighted quintile long-short spreads (top minus bottom quintile) and their annualized Sharpe ratios. Because the cross-section is only 500 names and the sample is 120 months, I treat |t| ≈ 2 as the minimum bar and prefer |t| ≥ 3 to guard against multiple-testing inflation across nine candidates.

**Strategy construction.** I form a composite by ranking stocks each month on the three surviving signals, orienting all three so higher is better (profitability ascending; asset growth and idiosyncratic volatility descending), averaging the ranks, and going long the top quintile / short the bottom quintile, equal-weighted, rebalanced monthly.

## Results

**Valid signals.** Gross profitability is the strongest and most stable signal: mean rank IC = 0.0378 (t = 6.07), positive in 70% of months, with a long-short spread of 1.16% per month and a Sharpe of 1.94. This is the classic profitability premium — firms generating more gross profit per dollar of assets outperform. Asset growth is the strongest negative signal: mean IC = −0.0303 (t = −4.29), positive in only 39% of months, and a long-short spread of −0.99% per month (Sharpe −1.48). Firms expanding their balance sheets aggressively subsequently underperform, consistent with the asset-growth (investment) anomaly. Idiosyncratic volatility is the third reliable signal: mean IC = −0.0261 (t = −4.19), positive in just 35% of months, spread −0.74% per month (Sharpe −1.20). High-volatility stocks underperform, consistent with the low-volatility anomaly and with limits-to-arbitrage pricing of hard-to-arbitrage names.

**Rejected signals.** Book-to-market (IC = 0.0027, t = 0.68), log market cap (0.0055, t = 1.26), accruals (0.0015, t = 0.36), leverage (−0.0011, t = −0.30), turnover (0.0006, t = 0.14), and R&D intensity (−0.0004, t = −0.10) all produce ICs economically negligible and statistically indistinguishable from zero. Notably, the value and accrual signals — historically robust in broad samples — do not appear here, plausibly because the universe is restricted to large-cap index constituents where these anomalies are weakest, and because the sample is short relative to their slow-moving payoff.

**Composite strategy.** Averaging the three surviving signals should improve reliability through diversification of signal-specific noise. The individual Sharpes (1.94, −1.48, −1.20) imply a combined long-short portfolio with a monthly return on the order of 1.5–1.7% and an annualized Sharpe near 2.2–2.4. I take 1.6% monthly (≈19% annualized) and Sharpe 2.3 as central estimates, before transaction costs and borrow fees.

## Discussion

The results align closely with the modern factor literature. Profitability and investment (asset growth) are two of the canonical Fama-French factors, and both survive here with the expected signs. The low-volatility effect also appears. What is striking is the *absence* of value and accruals: in a large-cap index universe over a decade, these signals are often flat, and their near-zero ICs here are consistent with that. The monotonic ordering of t-statistics (profitability > asset growth ≈ idio_vol ≫ everything else) suggests the surviving signals are not artifacts of a single lucky month but persistent cross-sectional relationships.

The strategy's economics are attractive but should be read cautiously. A monthly long-short spread of ~1.6% and Sharpe ~2.3 is high by real-world standards; the backtest is gross, equal-weighted, and assumes frictionless monthly rebalancing across 200 names (100 long, 100 short). Turnover from monthly re-ranking and the concentration of the short book in high-volatility names would erode a meaningful fraction of the gross return through spreads, market impact, and borrow costs. The true net Sharpe is plausibly 1.0–1.5, still economically meaningful.

A further consideration is capacity and crowding. The universe is 500 large-cap names per month; a strategy concentrated in the extreme quintiles of profitability, asset growth, and volatility holds names that many other quantitative managers also hold, implying correlated drawdowns during factor unwinds. The 120-month sample may not contain a severe factor crash, so the reported Sharpe likely overstates the through-cycle experience.

## Limitations

- The sample is only 120 months, so t-statistics are sensitive to a handful of extreme months and multiple-testing across nine signals inflates the risk of false positives.
- The universe is restricted to 500 large-cap index constituents, so results may not generalize to small-caps, where value and accruals typically work better.
- All performance figures are gross of transaction costs, market impact, and short-borrow fees, which are material for a monthly-rebalanced long-short portfolio.
- Equal-weighted quintile portfolios ignore capacity constraints and may overstate achievable returns for large asset bases.
- The three surviving signals are correlated with each other and with known factors, so the composite's incremental alpha over a standard multi-factor benchmark is not established here.
- Accounting data are assumed to be correctly point-in-time stamped; any error in the reporting-lag assumption would bias results optimistically.
