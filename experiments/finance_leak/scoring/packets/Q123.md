# A Robust Equity Signal: Standardized Unexpected Earnings in a Broad Market Index

## Abstract

This study examines the predictive power of ten firm characteristics for next-month stock returns in a monthly panel of 500 large-cap stocks over 120 months. Among the characteristics, only Standardized Unexpected Earnings (`sue`) demonstrates a statistically significant and economically meaningful rank information coefficient, with a mean monthly rank IC of 0.0998 and a t-statistic of 23.25. A long-short quintile portfolio based on `sue` generates an average monthly return of 3.04% with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 7.31. All other features exhibit economically negligible and statistically insignificant predictive power, suggesting that in this large-cap universe, earnings surprise is the dominant cross-sectional return signal.

## Methods

The analysis is conducted on a point-in-time monthly panel of 500 constituents of a broad equity index, spanning 120 months. Each month, ten characteristics are observed for each stock: book-to-market, gross profitability, log market capitalization, asset growth, accruals, standardized unexpected earnings, idiosyncratic volatility, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity. The target variable is the stock's total return over the subsequent month (`ret_next`).

For each characteristic, we compute the within-month Spearman rank correlation (rank IC) between the characteristic and `ret_next`. The mean rank IC across all 120 months, its standard deviation, and the resulting t-statistic (mean / std × √120) are reported. Additionally, we construct an equal-weighted long-short portfolio that buys the top quintile and sells the bottom quintile of each characteristic each month, and we report its mean monthly return and annualized Sharpe ratio. A feature is considered a valid signal if its rank IC t-statistic exceeds conventional significance thresholds (e.g., |t| > 2) and its long-short Sharpe ratio is economically meaningful.

## Results

The signal statistics reveal a stark contrast between `sue` and all other features.

**Standardized Unexpected Earnings (`sue`)** is an exceptionally strong predictor. Its mean rank IC is 0.0998 with a standard deviation of 0.0470, yielding a t-statistic of 23.25. The IC is positive in 99% of months, indicating near-universal consistency. The long-short quintile portfolio earns 3.04% per month on average, with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 7.31. This is consistent with the post-earnings-announcement drift literature: stocks with positive earnings surprises continue to outperform in the following month.

**All other features fail to show meaningful predictive power.** Their mean rank ICs are all within ±0.006, with t-statistics well below 2 in absolute value. For example, `asset_growth` has the next-highest t-statistic at 1.64 (mean IC 0.0061), followed by `accruals` at 1.34 (mean IC 0.0055). Their long-short monthly returns are 0.28% and 0.29% respectively, with annualized Sharpe ratios below 0.75. Features such as `book_to_market`, `gross_prof`, `log_mktcap`, `idio_vol`, `leverage`, `turnover`, and `rd_intensity` have even lower t-statistics (all |t| ≤ 1.14) and negligible long-short returns. Notably, `book_to_market` and `leverage` show negative but insignificant mean ICs (-0.0030 and -0.0043), while `log_mktcap`, `turnover`, and `rd_intensity` have mean ICs of essentially zero.

## Discussion

The results strongly indicate that, within this large-cap universe, the only robust cross-sectional signal for next-month returns is earnings surprise. The magnitude of the `sue` rank IC (≈0.10) is large relative to typical cross-sectional return predictors, which often exhibit monthly rank ICs of 0.02–0.05. The near-perfect consistency (99% positive IC months) and the high Sharpe ratio suggest that the signal is not driven by a few outliers or a single subperiod.

The absence of predictive power for value (`book_to_market`), size (`log_mktcap`), profitability (`gross_prof`), and investment (`asset_growth`) is notable. In broad cross-sections that include small-cap stocks, these factors often show significant spreads. Here, the universe is restricted to 500 large-cap index constituents, which may compress the cross-sectional variation in these characteristics and reduce their return spreads. Additionally, the sample period may be one in which value and size premia are weak or negative. The weak results for `accruals` and `idio_vol`—both of which are well-documented anomalies—are also consistent with a large-cap effect: institutional ownership and analyst coverage may arbitrage away these signals more efficiently in large caps.

The `sue` signal, however, remains strong even in large caps, likely because earnings surprises are harder to fully arbitrage immediately due to limits to arbitrage and the gradual diffusion of information. The strategy of buying high-`sue` stocks and selling low-`sue` stocks each month would have produced a monthly return of 3.04% before transaction costs. Given the high liquidity of large-cap stocks, a realistic implementation might retain a substantial portion of this gross return. However, the reported Sharpe ratio of 7.31 is likely overstated for a real-world strategy due to turnover, market impact, and the equal-weighting scheme; a more conservative estimate would be an annualized Sharpe of 2–3 after costs.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis is based solely on summary statistics and signal tables; no firm-level regressions or portfolio sorts with controls (e.g., double-sorted portfolios) were performed, so interactions among characteristics are not accounted for. Second, the sample is restricted to large-cap index constituents, limiting the generalizability of the null results for value, size, and other factors to smaller-cap universes. Third, the long-short returns are gross of transaction costs, which can be substantial for monthly rebalanced strategies. Fourth, the extraordinary Sharpe ratio for `sue` may be influenced by the specific sample period or by survivorship in the index; the point-in-time construction mitigates survivorship bias but does not eliminate it. Finally, the analysis assumes no look-ahead bias in the construction of `sue`, which relies on consensus forecasts and announcement dates; any misalignment could inflate the signal.
