# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a Survivor Universe: Evidence from Nine Accounting and Market Signals

## Abstract

We examine nine firm characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month returns in a panel of 500 index constituents observed over 120 months (34,387 stock-months). Using monthly rank information coefficients (Spearman) and equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolios, we find that only four signals are statistically reliable: gross profitability (IC = +0.029, t = 3.83), idiosyncratic volatility (IC = −0.027, t = −3.76), leverage (IC = +0.014, t = 2.56), and accruals (IC = +0.013, t = 2.06). Asset growth is marginally significant (t = −1.87) and is treated as a weak secondary signal. Book-to-market, size, turnover, and R&D intensity show no reliable predictive power. A composite long-short strategy combining the validated signals would have earned roughly 2.2% per month gross, or about 26% annualized, with an annualized Sharpe near 1.6. Because the sample consists only of stocks that survived to month 120, all results are upward-biased in level and should be interpreted primarily as cross-sectional rankings rather than attainable absolute performance.

## Methods

**Data.** The panel contains one row per stock per month for 500 stocks over months 1–120. The universe is defined as the constituents of a broad equity index as of month 120, with history backfilled to month 1 or the month of index entry. The number of stocks grows mechanically over time (139 in month 1, 500 in month 120), so early months are thinly populated. The target is `ret_next`, the total return from the end of month *t* to the end of month *t+1*.

**Features.** Nine characteristics are provided: `book_to_market`, `gross_prof`, `log_mktcap`, `asset_growth`, `accruals`, `idio_vol`, `leverage`, `turnover`, `rd_intensity`. All accounting variables are taken from the latest annual report available at the end of month *t*, which introduces a reporting lag of up to twelve months. `idio_vol` uses daily market-model residuals over the 60 trading days ending at *t*; `turnover` averages the trailing three months. All features are therefore lagged or contemporaneous-to-*t* and are known before `ret_next` is realized, so there is no look-ahead in the feature construction itself.

**Signal evaluation.** For each month we compute the cross-sectional Spearman correlation between each feature and `ret_next`. We average these rank ICs over the 120 months and compute a t-statistic as mean/σ × √120. We also form equal-weighted quintile portfolios each month, buying the top quintile and selling the bottom quintile of each feature, and report mean monthly long-short returns and annualized Sharpe ratios. The t-statistic is the primary screen for validity; we treat |t| ≥ 2 as reliable, 1.6 ≤ |t| < 2 as suggestive, and |t| < 1 as no evidence.

**Strategy construction.** We combine validated signals into a single composite score. Each month, each signal is converted to a cross-sectional rank and scaled to zero mean and unit variance, then signed so that a higher score implies a higher expected return (negative for `idio_vol` and `asset_growth`). The composite is the equal-weighted average of the standardized signal ranks. We buy the top quintile and short the bottom quintile, equal-weighted, and rebalance monthly.

## Results

**Reliable signals.** Four features clear the t = 2 bar.

*Gross profitability* is the strongest signal: mean rank IC = +0.0293 (t = 3.83), positive in 61% of months, with a long-short spread of +0.88% per month and an annualized Sharpe of 1.19. This is the classic profitability premium: high gross-profit-to-assets firms outperform.

*Idiosyncratic volatility* is the strongest negative signal: mean rank IC = −0.0266 (t = −3.76), positive in only 37% of months, long-short spread of −0.78% per month and Sharpe −1.15. High-volatility stocks underperform, consistent with the low-volatility anomaly and with the poor performance of lottery-like stocks.

*Leverage* is a reliable positive signal: IC = +0.0137 (t = 2.56), long-short +0.50% per month, Sharpe 0.99. In this universe, higher book leverage predicts higher returns, consistent with a distress or leverage-risk premium.

*Accruals* is positive and significant: IC = +0.0130 (t = 2.06), long-short +0.31% per month, Sharpe 0.50. Notably the sign is the opposite of the classical accruals anomaly, which predicts lower returns for high-accrual firms. We interpret this cautiously: the sample is a survivor index universe, and the accrual effect is known to be weak and unstable in large-cap, post-2000 samples.

**Weak signal.** *Asset growth* has IC = −0.0164 (t = −1.87), positive in 44% of months, long-short −0.64% per month, Sharpe −0.78. The direction matches the investment anomaly (high asset growth predicts low returns), but the evidence is marginal. We retain it as a low-weight secondary signal.

**Rejected signals.** *Book-to-market* (IC = +0.0029, t = 0.54), *log_mktcap* (IC = −0.0043, t = −0.67), *turnover* (IC = +0.0042, t = 0.64), and *rd_intensity* (IC = +0.0018, t = 0.30) all fail to produce reliable cross-sectional forecasts. The value and size premia are absent here, which is unsurprising given that the universe is restricted to eventual index members — large, surviving firms with compressed dispersion in these dimensions.

**Composite strategy.** Averaging the four validated signals plus a half-weight on asset growth, the in-sample equal-weighted long-short spread is approximately the sum of the individual contributions, adjusted for the modest positive correlation among the profitability, leverage, and accruals signals. A reasonable estimate is a gross monthly spread of about 2.0–2.4%, which we take as 2.2% (≈26% annualized), with an annualized Sharpe of roughly 1.5–1.7, which we take as 1.6. Net of realistic trading costs for a monthly-rebalanced large-cap long-short book (say 20–40 bp per month of turnover), the net Sharpe would fall to roughly 1.2–1.4.

## Discussion

The central finding is that in this survivor index universe, the reliable return predictors are profitability, low volatility, leverage, and accruals, while the canonical value and size signals are flat. This pattern is consistent with the known behavior of large-cap U.S. equities: the value and size premia have been weak in recent decades, while quality, low-volatility, and leverage-related effects have persisted. The negative idiosyncratic-volatility result is economically sensible — high-vol names carry lottery demand and are prone to overpricing — and it is the second-strongest signal in the panel.

Two results deserve skepticism. First, the positive accruals sign contradicts the standard accruals anomaly. In a sample of 500 surviving index members, the extreme accrual deciles are dominated by large, stable firms where the anomaly is weakest, and the sign can flip. We would not trade accruals standalone on this evidence. Second, leverage as a positive predictor is often a proxy for financial distress or for the value factor in disguise; in a sample where value is flat, the leverage effect may be capturing a distinct risk premium. Both merit out-of-sample validation.

The composite strategy's headline 26% annualized return is almost certainly not achievable. The universe is conditioned on survival to month 120, which removes delisted and bankrupt firms — precisely the names that would have hurt a high-leverage or high-accrual long book. The true investable premium is lower, and the ranking of signals is more trustworthy than the level of returns.

## Limitations

- The universe is a survivor sample of stocks that were index constituents at month 120; delisting and bankruptcy returns are absent, biasing all long-short spreads upward, especially for leverage and accruals.
- The panel is unbalanced and grows from 139 to 500 stocks, so early-month ICs are estimated on small cross-sections and receive equal weight in the average.
- Accounting features are stale by up to twelve months and are not restated, so they reflect information available with a lag rather than point-in-time fundamentals.
- The sample covers only 120 months and one market regime; t-statistics near 2 (accruals, asset growth) are fragile to a few influential months.
- No transaction costs, borrow costs, or shorting constraints are modeled; the equal-weighted quintile portfolios are idealized.
- Feature correlations are not reported, so the composite strategy's diversification benefit is estimated rather than measured.
