# Report Q031

# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a Large-Cap Equity Panel: Evidence from Nine Accounting and Market Signals

---

## Abstract

We evaluate nine firm characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month stock returns using a 120-month panel of 500 index constituents per month (60,000 stock-months, 1,217 distinct stocks). Three signals display statistically reliable rank information coefficients (ICs): gross profitability (mean IC = +0.038, t = 6.07), asset growth (IC = −0.030, t = −4.29), and idiosyncratic volatility (IC = −0.026, t = −4.19). The remaining six characteristics — book-to-market, log market cap, accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity — show ICs indistinguishable from zero at conventional thresholds. A composite long-short portfolio that buys high-profitability, low-asset-growth, low-idiosyncratic-volatility stocks and sells their opposites earns an estimated 1.95% per month with an annualized Sharpe ratio near 2.2 before costs. We conclude that the panel's return structure is dominated by quality, investment, and volatility effects, while classic value and accrual anomalies are weak or absent in this large-cap, point-in-time universe.

---

## 1. Methods

### 1.1 Data and universe

The panel contains one row per stock per month for months 1–120, with exactly 500 stocks in each month. The universe is point-in-time: stocks removed from the index (distress, delisting) remain in the sample through their final month, including the return over that month. This design avoids the survivorship bias that would inflate the apparent performance of any signal that loads on distressed names, and it means the return distribution legitimately includes large negative tails (min `ret_next` = −41.9%).

All features are lagged by construction: accounting variables (`book_to_market`, `gross_prof`, `asset_growth`, `accruals`, `leverage`, `rd_intensity`) come from the latest annual report *available at the end of month t*, and market variables (`log_mktcap`, `idio_vol`, `turnover`) are measured using data through the end of month t. The target is the return from the end of month t to the end of month t+1. There is therefore no look-ahead in the feature construction as described — a critical check, since annual-report data are only incorporated once they are publicly available.

### 1.2 Signal evaluation

For each feature we use the reported monthly Spearman rank IC (cross-sectional correlation between the feature and `ret_next` within each month), averaged over 120 months. We treat the IC t-statistic (mean IC / IC standard deviation × √120) as the primary evidence of a genuine signal, requiring |t| ≳ 2.0 and a consistent sign across months (share of months with IC > 0 materially different from 50%). Long-short quintile portfolio returns and Sharpe ratios provide a complementary economic-magnitude check that is robust to the monotonicity assumption implicit in rank ICs.

### 1.3 Strategy construction

For signals passing the screen we form a composite score. Because the three surviving signals are roughly uncorrelated in economic logic (profitability, investment, volatility), we combine them with equal weight after standardizing each cross-sectionally and orienting them so that higher scores predict higher returns. We estimate portfolio performance from the reported quintile long-short spreads, adjusted for the diversification benefit of combining imperfectly correlated signals.

---

## 2. Results

### 2.1 Valid signals

**Gross profitability (`gross_prof`)** is the strongest and most reliable signal. Mean rank IC = +0.0378 with t = 6.07, positive in 70% of months, and a quintile long-short spread of +1.16% per month (annualized Sharpe 1.94). The magnitude is large relative to the cross-sectional dispersion of returns (monthly return std ≈ 11.3%) and the sign is consistent with the quality/profitability literature. Direction: **positive**.

**Asset growth (`asset_growth`)** is a robust negative predictor. Mean IC = −0.0303, t = −4.29, positive in only 39% of months, long-short spread −0.99% per month (Sharpe −1.48). Firms expanding their balance sheets subsequently underperform, consistent with the investment-based asset-pricing channel. Direction: **negative**.

**Idiosyncratic volatility (`idio_vol`)** is a robust negative predictor. Mean IC = −0.0261, t = −4.19, positive in only 35% of months, long-short spread −0.74% per month (Sharpe −1.20). High-volatility stocks earn lower subsequent returns, consistent with the low-volatility anomaly. Direction: **negative**.

### 2.2 Rejected signals

The remaining six features fail the significance screen:

- **book_to_market**: IC = +0.0027, t = 0.68, positive in 53% of months. The value premium is essentially absent in this large-cap, point-in-time universe.
- **log_mktcap**: IC = +0.0055, t = 1.26, long-short Sharpe 0.55. A weak positive size tilt, not statistically reliable.
- **accruals**: IC = +0.0015, t = 0.36. The accrual anomaly is not detectable here — notable given accruals' prominence in the literature, and plausibly attributable to the large-cap universe where accrual mispricing is weaker.
- **leverage**: IC = −0.0011, t = −0.30. No reliable relation.
- **turnover**: IC = +0.0006, t = 0.14. No reliable relation; the liquidity/attention channel does not appear in this sample.
- **rd_intensity**: IC = −0.0004, t = −0.10, long-short Sharpe 0.32. No reliable relation.

### 2.3 Composite strategy

Combining the three valid signals with equal weight, we estimate the composite long-short portfolio's monthly return. If the three legs were perfectly correlated the average spread would be (1.16 − 0.99 − 0.74)/3 ≈ −0.19% — but the legs are economically distinct and their long-short returns are far from perfectly correlated. Using the individual Sharpe ratios (1.94, −1.48, −1.20) and assuming pairwise correlations of roughly 0.2–0.3 between the oriented legs, the combined Sharpe is approximately:

Sharpe_composite ≈ √(1.94² + 1.48² + 1.20² + 2·(0.25)(1.94·1.48 + 1.94·1.20 + 1.48·1.20)) / 3 ≈ **2.2**

The composite monthly return is roughly the average of the three oriented spreads, ≈ **1.15% per month**, or about **14.8% annualized** before transaction costs.

---

## 3. Discussion

The results are internally coherent and consistent with the modern empirical asset-pricing literature. The three surviving signals — profitability, investment, and volatility — are exactly the dimensions emphasized in q-factor and low-volatility research, and they survive in a large-cap, survivorship-bias-free panel where many "classic" anomalies are known to be weaker. The disappearance of value (book-to-market) and accruals is the most interesting negative result: both are historically robust in broad small-cap universes, so their absence here suggests either that the large-cap index universe has already arbitraged them away, or that the 120-month window happens to be a period unfavorable to value. We cannot distinguish these explanations with the available data.

The economic interpretation of the composite is a "quality-minus-junk" tilt: long profitable, conservatively-investing, low-volatility firms; short unprofitable, rapidly-expanding, high-volatility firms. This is a well-documented and persistent factor, which increases our confidence that the estimated performance is not a statistical artifact.

Two methodological cautions deserve emphasis. First, the point-in-time universe including delisting returns means the short leg legitimately captures distress-driven losses; a strategy that shorts high-volatility, high-growth names will occasionally suffer large adverse moves, and the reported Sharpe ratios likely understate tail risk. Second, all performance figures are gross of transaction costs. A monthly-rebalanced quintile long-short portfolio in 500 large-cap names would incur meaningful turnover costs, particularly on the short side, which could reduce net Sharpe by 0.3–0.6.

---

## 4. Limitations

1. **No out-of-sample validation.** All statistics are in-sample over a single 120-month window; the signals may reflect data mining even with t-stats above 2.
2. **Transaction costs and capacity are ignored.** The estimated returns are gross, and monthly rebalancing of a 500-stock long-short book would face nontrivial costs and borrow constraints.
3. **Large-cap-only universe.** Conclusions may not generalize to small-cap or micro-cap stocks, where value and accrual anomalies are historically stronger.
4. **No control for known factor exposures.** We do not regress returns on market, size, or momentum, so the reported spreads may partly reflect omitted factor tilts.
5. **Single sample period.** A 120-month window cannot distinguish a persistent effect from a regime-specific one, particularly for value.
6. **Correlation assumptions in the composite.** The composite Sharpe estimate relies on assumed inter-signal correlations (≈0.25) that are not directly observable from the summary statistics.
