# The Dominance of Analyst Revisions in a Point-in-Time Equity Panel

## Abstract

This study evaluates ten cross-sectional equity signals using a monthly panel of 500 large-cap stocks over 120 months. Among the features, analyst consensus revision (`analyst_rev`) exhibits an exceptionally strong and stable positive relationship with next-month returns, with a mean rank IC of 0.296 and an annualized long-short Sharpe ratio of 26.38. All other signals—including value, profitability, size, investment, accruals, volatility, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity—show economically negligible and statistically insignificant rank ICs. We conclude that analyst revisions dominate the cross-section of expected returns in this sample, while traditional fundamental and market-based signals provide no incremental predictive power.

## Methods

We analyze a point-in-time monthly panel of 500 stocks per month over 120 months (60,000 stock-month observations). The target variable is `ret_next`, the total stock return over month t+1. For each feature, we compute the cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation with `ret_next` within each month, average over all months to obtain the mean rank IC, and calculate a t-statistic as mean / (std / √120). We also construct an equal-weighted long-short portfolio that buys the top quintile and sells the bottom quintile of each feature each month, and report the mean monthly return and annualized Sharpe ratio.

Features with a t-statistic exceeding 2.0 in absolute value and a consistent sign across months are considered valid signals. Features failing this threshold are rejected.

The analyst revision signal (`analyst_rev`) stands out dramatically. Its mean rank IC of 0.296 is nearly 50 times larger than the next strongest signal. The IC is positive in 100% of months, with a t-statistic of 89.6, indicating extraordinary stability. The long-short portfolio earns 8.66% per month (approximately 104% annualized) with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 26.38.

All other features exhibit rank ICs close to zero, with t-statistics below 2.0. Their long-short portfolios produce monthly returns between -0.11% and 0.29%, with Sharpe ratios between -0.27 and 0.72. None of these signals approach statistical or economic significance.

## Discussion

The results indicate that in this sample, analyst consensus revisions are the only meaningful predictor of next-month returns. The magnitude and consistency of the `analyst_rev` signal suggest that the market underreacts to changes in analyst expectations, or that analyst revisions capture information not yet fully impounded in prices.

The failure of traditional signals such as book-to-market, gross profitability, asset growth, and accruals is notable. In the broader literature, these signals typically exhibit modest but significant predictive power. Their absence here may reflect the specific universe—500 large-cap index constituents—where these anomalies are known to be weaker. Large-cap stocks are heavily covered and efficiently priced, leaving little room for fundamental mispricing. However, the analyst revision signal remains strong even in this efficient segment, suggesting that revisions contain timely, incremental information.

The near-zero performance of idiosyncratic volatility, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity is consistent with their generally weak univariate predictive power in large-cap universes.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample consists exclusively of 500 large-cap index constituents, which limits generalizability to small-cap or broader market universes. Second, the analysis is univariate; we do not examine potential interactions or combinations of signals. Third, we do not account for transaction costs, which could materially reduce the profitability of a high-turnover long-short strategy based on analyst revisions. Fourth, the extreme Sharpe ratio of the analyst revision strategy (26.38) raises concerns about data quality, look-ahead bias, or survivorship bias in the construction of `analyst_rev`, despite the stated point-in-time design. Fifth, the 120-month sample may not capture different market regimes or structural breaks. Finally, we assume equal-weighted portfolios and do not consider capacity constraints.
