# Cross-Sectional Stock Return Predictors: Evidence from a Monthly Panel

## Abstract

We evaluate nine firm characteristics as predictors of next-month stock returns in a panel of 500 stocks over 120 months. Using monthly cross-sectional rank correlations and quintile long-short portfolios, we find that only leverage is a statistically robust signal: higher leverage predicts higher returns, with a mean rank IC of 0.0164 and an IC t-statistic of 2.74. A long-short portfolio that buys the top leverage quintile and sells the bottom leverage quintile earns 0.52% per month with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.92. The other eight characteristics do not have statistically significant predictive power. We propose a monthly rebalanced leverage quintile strategy with an expected annual return of approximately 6.2% before costs.

## Methods

The sample contains 34,708 stock-month observations covering 500 stocks over 120 months. The prediction target is `ret_next`, the total stock return over month t+1. For each feature, predictive power is assessed in two ways. First, we compute the monthly cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation between the feature and `ret_next`; the mean rank IC is the average across all 120 months, and the IC t-statistic is computed as mean / std × sqrt(120). Second, we form an equal-weighted long-short portfolio that buys the top quintile and sells the bottom quintile of the feature each month.

We define a valid signal as one whose mean rank IC is statistically distinguishable from zero at the conventional threshold of |t-stat| > 2.0. Features that do not meet this threshold are treated as not reliably predictive in this sample.

## Results

The table below summarizes the signal statistics.

| feature | mean rank IC | IC t-stat | long-short monthly return | annualized Sharpe |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| leverage | 0.0164 | 2.74 | 0.52% | 0.92 |
| asset_growth | 0.0055 | 1.16 | 0.37% | 0.74 |
| accruals | 0.0059 | 1.03 | 0.24% | 0.41 |
| idio_vol | 0.0056 | 0.86 | 0.13% | 0.19 |
| log_mktcap | 0.0014 | 0.26 | -0.07% | -0.14 |
| gross_prof | 0.0012 | 0.23 | 0.12% | 0.22 |
| rd_intensity | 0.0010 | 0.20 | -0.06% | -0.11 |
| turnover | -0.0005 | -0.10 | 0.13% | 0.23 |
| book_to_market | -0.0017 | -0.28 | -0.05% | -0.08 |

Only `leverage` has a mean rank IC with |t-stat| > 2.0. Its positive IC indicates that stocks with higher leverage tend to have higher next-month returns. The long-short leverage quintile portfolio produces a mean return of 0.52% per month, with an annualized Sharpe of 0.92. The remaining features have weak and statistically insignificant ICs, despite some showing modest long-short returns.

## Discussion

The finding that leverage positively predicts returns is consistent with a risk-based interpretation: more levered firms may be riskier and therefore earn higher expected returns. It may also reflect exposure to credit or distress risk, or correlation with omitted characteristics. In this sample, leverage is the only characteristic that clears the pre-specified statistical threshold.

Several other variables, such as asset growth and accruals, show economically small positive long-short returns but fail statistical significance. Value, profitability, size, idiosyncratic volatility, turnover, and R&D intensity do not exhibit reliable cross-sectional return prediction in this dataset.

Based on these results, we propose a monthly rebalanced, equal-weighted long-short strategy: buy the top 20% of stocks ranked by leverage and sell the bottom 20%. The in-sample expected performance is approximately 0.52% per month, or 6.2% annualized, with an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.92 before transaction costs and short-sale frictions.

## Limitations

- The universe is constructed from stocks that are index constituents at the end of the sample, which may introduce survivorship bias.
- The strategy returns are in-sample and do not account for transaction costs, short-selling fees, or liquidity constraints.
- With nine features tested, strict multiple-testing adjustments would weaken the statistical evidence for leverage.
- The analysis considers only linear rank relations and does not explore nonlinear or interaction effects.
- The signal may not persist out-of-sample or net of implementation costs.
