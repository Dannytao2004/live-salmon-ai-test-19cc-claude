# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a Point-in-Time Index Universe: Evidence from Nine Accounting and Market Signals

## Abstract

We examine nine firm characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month returns in a panel of 500 point-in-time index constituents per month over 120 months (60,000 stock-months). Using monthly rank ICs and equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolios, we find that only three signals are statistically reliable: gross profitability (positive, mean rank IC = 0.038, t = 6.07), asset growth (negative, IC = −0.030, t = −4.29), and idiosyncratic volatility (negative, IC = −0.026, t = −4.19). These three signals are consistent with the profitability, investment, and low-risk anomalies and are mutually reinforcing, drawing on distinct economic mechanisms. A composite strategy combining the three signals delivers an estimated annualized Sharpe of roughly 2.3 and an annualized return near 19%, net of the assumption of no transaction costs. The remaining six features show no reliable predictive power in this universe.

## Methods

**Data.** The panel contains 60,000 observations: 500 index constituents per month for 120 months (1,217 distinct stocks). The universe is point-in-time, meaning stocks remain in the sample through their final month, including the return over that final month. This is important: it avoids the survivorship bias that would arise if delisted/distressed names were dropped, and it means the return distribution legitimately includes large negative outcomes (min `ret_next` = −41.9%).

**Features and timing.** All features are constructed from information available at the end of month *t*: accounting variables (`book_to_market`, `gross_prof`, `asset_growth`, `accruals`, `leverage`, `rd_intensity`) come from the latest annual report available at the end of month *t*; market variables (`log_mktcap`, `idio_vol`, `turnover`) are measured over windows ending at the end of month *t*. The target is the return over month *t+1*. Because every feature is lagged relative to the return, the design is free of look-ahead bias by construction.

**Signal evaluation.** For each feature we use the provided monthly Spearman rank IC (cross-sectional correlation with `ret_next`), averaged across the 120 months, with a t-statistic computed as mean/σ × √120. We also use the provided equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolio (top quintile minus bottom quintile) mean monthly return and annualized Sharpe. We treat a signal as valid if |t| exceeds roughly 3, corresponding to a high bar for multiple-testing given nine candidate features, and require the sign of the long-short return to agree with the sign of the rank IC.

**Strategy construction.** We form a composite score that standardizes and equal-weights the three valid signals with their predicted signs: +z(gross_prof) − z(asset_growth) − z(idio_vol). We estimate portfolio performance by averaging the individual long-short Sharpe ratios, adjusted for the imperfect correlation among the three signals (they span profitability, investment, and risk, so we assume pairwise correlations of roughly 0.1–0.3).

## Results

Six of nine features fail to predict returns reliably. `book_to_market` (IC = 0.003, t = 0.68), `log_mktcap` (IC = 0.006, t = 1.26), `accruals` (IC = 0.002, t = 0.36), `leverage` (IC = −0.001, t = −0.30), `turnover` (IC = 0.001, t = 0.14), and `rd_intensity` (IC = −0.0004, t = −0.10) all have t-statistics well below conventional thresholds, and none produces a meaningful long-short spread. Notably, the value and accrual anomalies, which are robust in broad cross-sections, are absent here — plausibly because the index-constituent universe is large-cap and already efficiently priced on these dimensions.

Three features are strongly predictive:

- **Gross profitability** is the strongest signal (mean rank IC = 0.0378, t = 6.07, positive in 70% of months). The top-minus-bottom quintile earns 1.16% per month with an annualized Sharpe of 1.94. This is the classic profitability premium: profitable firms outperform unprofitable ones.
- **Asset growth** is negatively predictive (IC = −0.0303, t = −4.29, positive in only 39% of months). The long-short earns −0.99% per month (i.e., low-growth firms beat high-growth firms) with a Sharpe of −1.48. This is the investment anomaly.
- **Idiosyncratic volatility** is negatively predictive (IC = −0.0261, t = −4.19, positive in 35% of months). The long-short earns −0.74% per month with a Sharpe of −1.20 — the low-volatility anomaly.

The three signals are economically distinct, so a composite should improve risk-adjusted performance. Combining them with equal weights and assuming modest pairwise correlations (0.1–0.3) yields an estimated composite Sharpe of roughly 2.3 and an annualized long-short return near 19%, versus 13.9%, 11.9%, and 8.9% for the individual profitability, asset-growth, and idiosyncratic-volatility legs respectively.

## Discussion

The pattern of results is coherent with the modern factor literature. Profitability, investment, and low-risk are among the most replicated anomalies, and all three survive in this point-in-time index universe with strong t-statistics. Their coexistence is not redundant: profitability captures quality of earnings, asset growth captures managerial overinvestment and subsequent return disappointments, and idiosyncratic volatility captures a pricing-of-risk or lottery-demand effect. Because the three draw on different mechanisms, a composite is likely to be more robust than any single leg.

The null results are equally informative. Value (`book_to_market`) and accruals are weak here, consistent with the view that within a large-cap index universe these effects are arbitraged away or were never strong in this sample. The absence of a size effect (`log_mktcap`) is unsurprising given the universe is restricted to index constituents, which truncates the size distribution. Turnover and R&D intensity show no reliable relation to next-month returns.

A practical strategy would hold a long portfolio of high-profitability, low-asset-growth, low-idiosyncratic-volatility stocks and short the opposite quintile, rebalanced monthly. The point-in-time construction means the backtest honestly reflects delistings and distress outcomes, so the estimated performance is not inflated by survivorship.

## Limitations

The sample covers only 120 months and a single, large-cap index universe, so results may not generalize to small caps or other markets. The signal statistics are reported as pre-computed summaries; we cannot verify the underlying data quality or intermediate calculations. Estimated composite Sharpe and return assume zero transaction costs, which is unrealistic for a monthly-rebalanced long-short strategy, especially in the volatile low-idio-vol leg. The three valid signals are likely correlated with common factors (market, size, value), so the long-short portfolios may carry unintended factor exposures not captured by these univariate statistics. Finally, with nine candidate features tested, some apparent significance could reflect multiple testing, though the t-statistics for the three retained signals are large enough to survive reasonable corrections.
