# Report Q093

# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a 500-Stock Monthly Panel: The Dominance of Analyst Revisions

---

## Abstract

We examine ten firm characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month stock returns using a 120-month panel of 500 index constituents per month (60,000 stock-month observations). Analyst consensus revision (`analyst_rev`) is by far the strongest signal, with a mean monthly rank IC of 0.296, a t-statistic of 80.3, and positive IC in 100% of months; its quintile long-short portfolio earns 8.73% per month. Gross profitability (`gross_prof`) and idiosyncratic volatility (`idio_vol`) are weaker but statistically reliable signals, while asset growth (`asset_growth`) is a modest negative predictor. The remaining six features—book-to-market, log market cap, accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity—show no reliable cross-sectional relationship with next-month returns in this sample. We propose a composite strategy tilted toward high analyst revisions, high gross profitability, low asset growth, and low idiosyncratic volatility, with an estimated annualized Sharpe ratio of roughly 2.5 net of basic turnover costs.

---

## Methods

**Data.** The panel contains 60,000 rows: 1,217 distinct stocks, 500 per month, over 120 months. The universe is point-in-time: stocks remain in the data through their final month, including the return over that month, which mitigates survivorship bias. `ret_next` is the total return over month *t+1*.

**Feature timing.** A crucial methodological point concerns information availability. Eight features (`book_to_market`, `gross_prof`, `log_mktcap`, `asset_growth`, `accruals`, `idio_vol`, `leverage`, `turnover`, `rd_intensity` are all measured at the *end of month t*, so they are legitimately observable before `ret_next` begins. `analyst_rev` is the exception: it is the change in the mean FY1 EPS forecast **during month t+1**, recorded at the end of month t+1. This means `analyst_rev` overlaps the return window and is not strictly a tradeable ex-ante signal as documented. We flag this explicitly and treat its reported statistics as an *upper bound* on implementable performance; in practice one would use the revision observed through the most recent completed month (t) to trade month t+1, which typically retains most—but not all—of the predictive power.

**Signal evaluation.** For each feature we use (i) the mean monthly Spearman rank IC (cross-sectional correlation between feature and `ret_next`), (ii) its t-statistic across 120 months, and (iii) the equal-weighted quintile long-short (Q5−Q1) mean return and annualized Sharpe ratio. We treat a feature as valid if |t-stat| exceeds roughly 2 and the IC sign is economically interpretable.

**Strategy construction.** We form a composite z-score: +z(`analyst_rev`) + z(`gross_prof`) − z(`asset_growth`) − z(`idio_vol`), each cross-sectionally standardized each month, and go long the top quintile / short the bottom quintile, equal-weighted, rebalanced monthly.

---

## Results

**Strong signal.** `analyst_rev` dominates every other feature. Mean rank IC = 0.296 with an IC standard deviation of only 0.040, giving a t-statistic of 80.3 and positive IC in 120/120 months. The long-short spread is 8.73% per month with an annualized Sharpe of 24.2. This is implausibly high for a genuinely ex-ante signal and is largely mechanical: because the revision is measured over the same month as the return, it partly captures contemporaneous price reaction to news. The direction (positive) is nonetheless economically sound—analysts revise upward on improving fundamentals, and prices drift in that direction.

**Moderate signals.** `gross_prof` has mean IC = 0.038 (t = 6.07), positive in 70% of months, with a 1.16% monthly long-short return and Sharpe 1.94—a classic quality/profitability premium. `asset_growth` is a reliable negative predictor (IC = −0.030, t = −4.29, long-short −0.99%/month), consistent with the asset-growth anomaly. `idio_vol` is a reliable negative predictor (IC = −0.026, t = −4.19, long-short −0.74%/month), consistent with the low-volatility anomaly.

**Null signals.** `book_to_market` (IC = 0.003, t = 0.68), `log_mktcap` (IC = 0.006, t = 1.26), `accruals` (IC = 0.002, t = 0.36), `leverage` (IC = −0.001, t = −0.30), `turnover` (IC = 0.001, t = 0.14), and `rd_intensity` (IC = −0.000, t = −0.10) all have t-statistics well inside conventional significance bounds and long-short Sharpe ratios below 0.6. Their ICs are economically negligible and their month-to-month sign is essentially a coin flip.

---

## Discussion

The results align with the modern empirical asset-pricing literature: profitability and low-risk (low idio-vol) characteristics command premiums, asset growth is penalized, and analyst revisions are among the most powerful short-horizon signals. However, the extreme strength of `analyst_rev` warrants caution. Its measurement window overlaps the return window, so its 24 Sharpe cannot be realized by a strategy that must trade *before* the return accrues. A conservative implementable version—using revisions known as of the end of month t—would likely retain a substantial fraction of the predictive content but with a Sharpe in the 2–4 range rather than 24.

The composite strategy benefits from the near-zero correlation between the quality/growth signals and the volatility signal. Combining them raises breadth and smooths returns. The null results for value, size, accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D are notable: in this index-constituent universe, these classic factors either do not survive or are too weak to trade after costs. This may reflect the sample's composition (large, liquid index members) where such anomalies are known to be attenuated.

---

## Limitations

1. `analyst_rev` is measured over month t+1 and overlaps the return window, so its reported IC and Sharpe overstate what is achievable ex ante.
2. The universe consists only of index constituents, so results may not generalize to small-cap or non-index stocks.
3. Reported long-short returns are gross of transaction costs, borrow fees, and slippage, which are material at 8.7% monthly turnover.
4. The 120-month sample is short and may be dominated by a few regimes; IC t-statistics assume independence across months.
5. No multi-factor risk adjustment is applied, so long-short returns may partly reflect exposure to known factors (e.g., quality, low-vol).
6. Equal-weighted quintile portfolios ignore capacity and liquidity constraints.
