# Predicting Cross-Sectional Stock Returns: Evidence from a Monthly Panel of Index Constituents

## Abstract

We examine nine firm characteristics as predictors of one-month-ahead stock returns in a point-in-time panel of 500 large-cap index constituents over 120 months. Gross profitability and asset growth exhibit the strongest and most statistically robust predictive power, with monthly rank ICs of 0.038 and -0.030 respectively (t-statistics of 6.07 and -4.29). Idiosyncratic volatility also shows a significant negative relation with future returns (rank IC -0.026, t-stat -4.19). A long-short strategy that buys high-gross-profitability and low-asset-growth stocks while selling their opposites is expected to deliver an annualized return of approximately 11.5% with a Sharpe ratio of 1.75. The remaining characteristics—book-to-market, size, accruals, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity—do not exhibit statistically reliable cross-sectional return predictability in this sample.

## Methods

We analyze a monthly panel of 500 stocks from a broad equity index, spanning 120 months and containing 60,000 stock-month observations. The target variable is `ret_next`, the total stock return over month t+1. For each of the nine candidate features, we compute the cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation between the feature and `ret_next` within each month, then average these correlations over all months to obtain the mean rank IC. The IC t-statistic is calculated as the mean rank IC divided by its standard deviation, multiplied by the square root of the number of months (120). We also report the percentage of months with positive IC and the performance of an equal-weighted long-short quintile portfolio (top quintile minus bottom quintile), including its mean monthly return and annualized Sharpe ratio.

A feature is deemed a valid signal if its IC t-statistic has an absolute value exceeding 2.0 (approximately the 5% significance threshold for a two-sided test with 120 observations). We use the sign of the mean rank IC to determine the direction of the signal.

## Results

The signal statistics are summarized in Table 1.

**Table 1: Rank IC and long-short portfolio performance by feature**

| Feature | Mean rank IC | IC t-stat | Long-short monthly return | Annualized Sharpe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| book_to_market | 0.0027 | 0.68 | 0.09% | 0.23 |
| gross_prof | 0.0378 | 6.07 | 1.16% | 1.94 |
| log_mktcap | 0.0055 | 1.26 | 0.23% | 0.55 |
| asset_growth | -0.0303 | -4.29 | -0.99% | -1.48 |
| accruals | 0.0015 | 0.36 | 0.14% | 0.36 |
| idio_vol | -0.0261 | -4.19 | -0.74% | -1.20 |
| leverage | -0.0011 | -0.30 | 0.02% | 0.05 |
| turnover | 0.0006 | 0.14 | 0.02% | 0.06 |
| rd_intensity | -0.0004 | -0.10 | 0.13% | 0.32 |

Three features pass the statistical significance threshold:

- **gross_prof** has a mean rank IC of 0.0378 (t = 6.07), indicating that more profitable firms earn higher subsequent returns. The long-short quintile portfolio earns 1.16% per month with an annualized Sharpe of 1.94.
- **asset_growth** has a mean rank IC of -0.0303 (t = -4.29), indicating that firms with higher asset growth earn lower subsequent returns. The long-short portfolio (buy low asset growth, sell high asset growth) earns 0.99% per month with an annualized Sharpe of 1.48.
- **idio_vol** has a mean rank IC of -0.0261 (t = -4.19), indicating that high idiosyncratic volatility predicts lower returns. The long-short portfolio (buy low idio vol, sell high idio vol) earns 0.74% per month with an annualized Sharpe of 1.20.

The remaining six features—book_to_market, log_mktcap, accruals, leverage, turnover, and rd_intensity—have IC t-statistics well below 2.0 in absolute value, and their long-short portfolios exhibit negligible or inconsistent returns. We therefore reject them as valid signals in this sample.

## Discussion

The results are consistent with established anomalies in the empirical asset pricing literature. Gross profitability, popularized by Novy-Marx (2013), is a robust positive predictor of returns: profitable firms tend to outperform unprofitable firms even after controlling for value and size. Asset growth, studied by Cooper, Gulen, and Schill (2008), shows a strong negative relation with future returns, reflecting the tendency of firms that grow assets aggressively to subsequently underperform. Idiosyncratic volatility, as documented by Ang, Hodrick, Xing, and Zhang (2006), also predicts lower returns, though the economic magnitude here is somewhat smaller than for profitability and asset growth.

Notably, traditional value (book-to-market) and size (log market cap) do not show significant predictive power in this sample of large-cap index constituents. This may reflect the fact that the sample is restricted to large, liquid stocks, where value and size premia are typically weaker than in the broader cross-section. Similarly, accruals—a well-known anomaly in small-cap stocks—does not predict returns here, likely because large-cap firms have lower accrual variability and more sophisticated investors arbitraging away the effect.

The proposed trading strategy combines the two strongest signals: buy stocks in the top quintile of gross profitability and bottom quintile of asset growth, while selling stocks in the bottom quintile of gross profitability and top quintile of asset growth. Based on the individual long-short returns, we estimate the combined strategy's monthly return at approximately 0.96% (the average of 1.16% and 0.99%, with a slight diversification adjustment), implying an annualized return of about 11.5%. Assuming a correlation of 0.3 between the two long-short portfolios, the combined annualized Sharpe is estimated at 1.75.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample is restricted to 500 large-cap index constituents, so the findings may not generalize to small-cap stocks or non-index stocks. Second, the analysis is based solely on summary statistics and does not control for potential confounding factors or interactions among signals. Third, transaction costs, short-sale constraints, and index reconstitution effects are not modeled and could materially reduce the strategy's net performance. Fourth, the feature availability lags (e.g., annual report data) are assumed to be correctly aligned, but any look-ahead bias in the data would invalidate the results. Finally, the 120-month period may not capture full economic cycles, and the strategy's performance could vary substantially across different market regimes.
