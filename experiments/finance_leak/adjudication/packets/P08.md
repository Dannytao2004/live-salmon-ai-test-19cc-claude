# Cross-Sectional Return Prediction in a Large-Cap Equity Panel: The Dominance of Earnings Surprise

## Abstract

We examine ten firm characteristics as cross-sectional predictors of next-month stock returns in a panel of 500 large-cap index constituents observed over 120 months (60,000 stock-months). Only standardized unexpected earnings (`sue`) exhibits a robust, economically meaningful relationship with future returns, with a mean monthly rank IC of 0.0998 (t-stat = 23.25) and a long-short quintile spread of 3.04% per month (annualized Sharpe 7.31). All nine other characteristics produce rank ICs of magnitude below 0.007 with t-statistics under 2, consistent with noise. We conclude that within this universe the earnings-announcement signal is the sole exploitable predictor, and we propose a long-short `sue` strategy with an estimated annualized return near 43% before costs. We caution that the extreme Sharpe ratio likely reflects an idealized, frictionless backtest and survivorship-controlled universe.

## Methods

**Data.** The panel contains 60,000 observations: 500 stocks per month across 120 months. Each row provides ten point-in-time characteristics measured at the end of month *t* and the total return over month *t+1* (`ret_next`). The universe is the set of index constituents in each month, with delisted/distressed names retained through their final month, which mitigates survivorship bias.

**Signal evaluation.** For each feature we use the reported monthly Spearman rank information coefficient (IC) between the feature and `ret_next`, averaged across the 120 months. We assess statistical reliability via the IC t-statistic (mean IC / standard deviation of IC × √120) and the hit rate (share of months with positive IC). We also use the reported equal-weighted top-quintile-minus-bottom-quintile long-short portfolio returns and annualized Sharpe ratios.

**Decision rule.** We treat a feature as a valid signal only if it shows (i) a mean |rank IC| that is economically non-trivial, (ii) a t-statistic indicating significance at conventional levels, and (iii) a long-short spread consistent in sign with the IC. Features failing these criteria are classified as noise. We deliberately apply a stringent bar given the multiple-testing problem of screening ten signals.

**Strategy construction.** For the surviving signal we form a monthly equal-weighted long-short portfolio: long the top quintile, short the bottom quintile, rebalanced monthly. Expected performance is anchored to the reported long-short mean and Sharpe, with a haircut discussed below.

**The `sue` signal.** Standardized unexpected earnings is by far the strongest predictor. Its mean rank IC of 0.0998 is roughly 16× larger than the next-best feature and its t-statistic of 23.25 is decisive. The IC is positive in 99% of months, indicating a highly stable, positive (momentum-like) relationship: stocks with positive earnings surprises subsequently outperform. The long-short quintile portfolio earns 3.04% per month with an annualized Sharpe of 7.31. Direction is **positive**.

**Other signals.** No other feature reaches a t-statistic of 2. `asset_growth` (positive, t = 1.64) and `accruals` (positive, t = 1.34) are the closest to significance, but their ICs (~0.006) are an order of magnitude smaller than `sue` and their long-short spreads (0.28–0.29%/month) are within plausible noise. Notably, the sign of `accruals` is opposite to the classic "accrual anomaly" (which predicts negative), and `book_to_market` is weakly negative rather than the positive value premium, suggesting these effects are absent or reversed in this large-cap, index-constrained universe.

**Strategy.** Long top-quintile `sue`, short bottom-quintile `sue`, equal-weighted, monthly rebalance. Reported long-short mean = 3.04%/month, Sharpe = 7.31.

## Discussion

The results are striking in their concentration: a single signal, earnings surprise, carries essentially all of the cross-sectional predictive power in this panel, while nine well-known "anomaly" characteristics are indistinguishable from noise. This is consistent with the post-earnings-announcement-drift (PEAD) literature, which documents that earnings surprises are followed by continued abnormal returns. The near-perfect monthly hit rate (99%) and very high Sharpe suggest the signal is not merely statistically significant but remarkably persistent in this sample.

Two features of the setting plausibly explain why the other signals fail. First, the universe is restricted to 500 large-cap index constituents, where size, value, profitability, and accrual effects are known to be weak or arbitraged away. Second, several characteristics (e.g., `book_to_market`, `leverage`) are slow-moving annual-report variables, and their point-in-time measurement may be too coarse to generate month-ahead cross-sectional dispersion in a 500-name universe.

The magnitude of the reported Sharpe (7.31) is implausibly high for a live strategy and should be interpreted as an upper bound. Real-world frictions—transaction costs, shorting constraints, capacity limits, and the fact that top-quintile `sue` names may be small or illiquid within the index—will erode much of the gross return. A more conservative expectation is a substantial haircut to both return and Sharpe.

## Limitations

- We rely on summary statistics rather than raw data, so we cannot inspect nonlinearities, interactions, or tail behavior.
- The universe is a fixed 500-name large-cap index, so findings may not generalize to small-cap or broad-market universes.
- Reported long-short returns are gross and equal-weighted, ignoring transaction costs, borrow costs, and capacity constraints.
- The very high `sue` Sharpe and 99% hit rate suggest possible look-ahead or data-snooping concerns that cannot be verified from the summary alone.
- Only linear rank IC and quintile sorts are reported; no risk-model adjustment (e.g., sector, beta) is applied, so part of the `sue` spread may reflect unmodeled risk exposures.
- The 120-month sample covers a single (unspecified) historical regime; stability out-of-sample is untested.
