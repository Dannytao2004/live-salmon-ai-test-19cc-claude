# Report P05

Title: Evaluating Cross-Sectional Equity Signals in a Monthly Stock Panel

Abstract  
We examine nine firm characteristics as potential predictors of next-month stock returns using a panel of 500 stocks over 120 months. Among the candidates, only leverage exhibits a statistically significant rank correlation with future returns, with higher leverage predicting higher returns. A long-short quintile strategy based on leverage yields a monthly return of 0.52% and an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.92. The remaining eight characteristics show no reliable predictive power, with rank ICs near zero and t-statistics below conventional thresholds. We discuss economic interpretations and limitations of the findings.

Methods  
We use a monthly panel of 34,708 stock-month observations covering 500 unique stocks over 120 months. For each characteristic, we compute the cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation with next-month returns (ret_next) within each month, then average across months to obtain the mean rank IC. The t-statistic is calculated as the mean rank IC divided by its standard deviation, multiplied by the square root of the number of months. A characteristic is considered a valid signal if its rank IC t-statistic exceeds 2.0 in absolute value and the sign is economically consistent. We also construct equal-weighted long-short portfolios that buy the top quintile and sell the bottom quintile of each characteristic, rebalanced monthly, and report the mean monthly return and annualized Sharpe ratio.

Results  
Table 1 summarizes the signal statistics. Leverage is the only feature with a statistically significant rank IC (mean = 0.0164, t-stat = 2.74, 59% of months positive). Its long-short portfolio earns 0.52% per month with an annualized Sharpe of 0.92. All other features have rank IC t-statistics below 1.2 in absolute value, indicating no reliable cross-sectional relationship with future returns. Notably, book-to-market, gross profitability, market capitalization, asset growth, accruals, idiosyncratic volatility, turnover, and R&D intensity all fail to predict returns in this sample.

Discussion  
The positive association between leverage and future returns is consistent with risk-based explanations: higher-leverage firms are riskier and may earn higher expected returns as compensation. However, the absence of predictive power for well-known anomalies such as book-to-market, gross profitability, and asset growth is surprising and may reflect the specific universe (current index constituents with survivorship bias) or the sample period. The long-short leverage strategy appears economically meaningful, though its Sharpe ratio is moderate.

Limitations  
The dataset is subject to survivorship bias because the universe consists of stocks that are index constituents at the end of month 120, excluding firms that were delisted or dropped earlier. The sample period is relatively short (10 years), and the results may not generalize to other markets or periods. Transaction costs and portfolio constraints are not considered. The rank IC t-statistic assumes independent monthly observations, which may be violated by overlapping annual report data and persistent firm characteristics.
