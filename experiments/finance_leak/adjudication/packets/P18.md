## Predictive Signals in a Monthly Stock Panel: Evaluating Nine Candidate Characteristics

### Abstract
We evaluate nine stock-level characteristics as predictors of next-month returns in a panel of 500 stocks over 120 months. Using monthly rank ICs and quintile long-short portfolio returns, we find that leverage is the only robustly significant signal, with a mean rank IC of 0.0164 and a t-statistic of 2.74. All other features exhibit weak and statistically insignificant predictive power. A long-short strategy based on leverage achieves an annualized Sharpe ratio of 0.92 with a monthly long-short return of 0.52%. The results suggest that leverage positively predicts returns in this sample, contrary to some prior literature on financial distress risk.

### Methods
We analyze a monthly panel of 34,708 stock-month observations covering 500 stocks over 120 months. The dataset includes nine candidate features: book-to-market, gross profitability, log market capitalization, asset growth, accruals, idiosyncratic volatility, leverage, turnover, and R&D intensity. The target variable is next-month return (`ret_next`).

For each feature, we compute the cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation (rank IC) between the feature and next-month return within each month, then average across all 120 months. We report the mean rank IC, its standard deviation, t-statistic (mean / std × √120), the percentage of months with positive IC, and the performance of a quintile long-short portfolio (long top quintile, short bottom quintile, equal-weighted, rebalanced monthly). A feature is considered a valid signal if its rank IC t-statistic exceeds 2.0 in absolute value. We also estimate the expected annual return and Sharpe ratio of a strategy based on the valid signal.

### Results
Table 1 summarizes the signal statistics for all nine features.

| Feature | Mean Rank IC | IC t-stat | Long-Short Monthly Return | Long-Short Annualized Sharpe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| book_to_market | -0.0017 | -0.28 | -0.05% | -0.08 |
| gross_prof | 0.0012 | 0.23 | 0.12% | 0.22 |
| log_mktcap | 0.0014 | 0.26 | -0.07% | -0.14 |
| asset_growth | 0.0055 | 1.16 | 0.37% | 0.74 |
| accruals | 0.0059 | 1.03 | 0.24% | 0.41 |
| idio_vol | 0.0056 | 0.86 | 0.13% | 0.19 |
| leverage | 0.0164 | 2.74 | 0.52% | 0.92 |
| turnover | -0.0005 | -0.10 | 0.13% | 0.23 |
| rd_intensity | 0.0010 | 0.20 | -0.06% | -0.11 |

Leverage is the only feature with a statistically significant rank IC (t = 2.74), and it also has the highest long-short Sharpe ratio (0.92). The positive mean IC indicates that stocks with higher leverage tend to have higher subsequent returns. The long-short portfolio based on leverage generates an average monthly return of 0.52%, corresponding to approximately 6.4% annualized (assuming monthly compounding). All other features have t-statistics below 1.2, indicating no reliable predictive power in this sample. Notably, accruals and asset growth show weak positive ICs (0.0059 and 0.0055) but fail to reach significance. Book-to-market, gross profitability, log market cap, idiosyncratic volatility, turnover, and R&D intensity show essentially zero predictive ability.

### Discussion
The positive and significant relation between leverage and future returns is the central finding of this study. This result is somewhat surprising given the traditional view that high leverage is associated with financial distress risk and lower expected returns (e.g., the leverage effect in asset pricing). However, our sample consists of stocks that are constituents of a broad equity index at the end of the sample period, which introduces survivorship bias: stocks that survived until month 120 may have benefited from leverage during the sample period. Additionally, the positive relation could reflect a risk premium for leverage-related distress risk, or it may be a manifestation of the value premium if leverage is correlated with book-to-market (though book-to-market itself is not significant here).

The lack of significance for other well-documented anomalies (e.g., book-to-market, gross profitability, asset growth, accruals) may be due to the relatively short sample (120 months), the survivorship-biased universe, or the specific definitions of the features. The weak positive ICs for asset growth and accruals are directionally consistent with some prior literature (asset growth negatively predicts returns, accruals negatively predict returns), but the signs here are positive, suggesting that the usual anomaly patterns do not hold in this sample. This could be due to the sample construction or the fact that these variables are measured with noise.

### Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, the sample is subject to survivorship bias because the universe is defined as stocks that are index constituents at the end of the sample period; stocks that were delisted or dropped from the index are excluded, which may bias the results. Second, the sample period is relatively short (120 months), limiting statistical power to detect weak signals. Third, the features are constructed from annual report data that may be stale relative to the monthly observation date, potentially introducing measurement error. Fourth, the analysis is based solely on summary statistics and rank ICs without controlling for correlations among features or adjusting for multiple testing. Fifth, the long-short portfolio returns do not account for transaction costs, which could significantly reduce the profitability of a leverage-based strategy, especially if turnover is high. Finally, the positive leverage-return relation may not be robust out-of-sample or in a broader universe.
