# The Dominance of Analyst Revisions in Cross-Sectional Stock Returns

## Abstract

We examine the predictive power of ten firm characteristics for next-month stock returns using a panel of 60,000 stock-month observations from a broad equity index. Among the ten features, only analyst consensus revision exhibits a statistically significant and economically meaningful relationship with future returns, with a mean rank IC of 0.296 and a t-statistic of 89.6. All other features show rank ICs near zero with t-statistics below 2.0, suggesting no reliable predictive power in this sample. A long-short quintile portfolio sorted on analyst revisions would have delivered an annualized Sharpe ratio of 26.4, an extraordinarily high value that warrants careful interpretation. We discuss the implications of this finding, including the possibility of look-ahead bias in the analyst revision variable.

## Methods

We analyze a monthly panel of 500 stocks over 120 months, for a total of 60,000 stock-month observations. The target variable is `ret_next`, the total stock return over month t+1. The ten candidate features are `book_to_market`, `gross_prof`, `log_mktcap`, `asset_growth`, `accruals`, `analyst_rev`, `idio_vol`, `leverage`, `turnover`, and `rd_intensity`.

For each feature, we compute the cross-sectional Spearman rank correlation with `ret_next` within each month, then average these correlations across the 120 months to obtain the mean rank IC. The IC t-statistic is computed as the mean IC divided by the standard deviation of the monthly ICs, multiplied by the square root of 120. We also construct equal-weighted long-short portfolios that buy the top quintile and sell the bottom quintile of each feature each month, and report the mean monthly return and annualized Sharpe ratio.

We define a signal as "valid" if its rank IC t-statistic exceeds 2.0 in absolute value and its long-short portfolio shows a consistent sign with the IC.

The signal statistics are summarized in Table 1.

| gross_prof | 0.0039 | 0.93 | 0.10% | 0.26 |

| asset_growth | 0.0061 | 1.64 | 0.28% | 0.72 |
| accruals | 0.0055 | 1.34 | 0.29% | 0.70 |
| **analyst_rev** | **0.2958** | **89.59** | **8.66%** | **26.38** |
| idio_vol | 0.0043 | 1.14 | 0.03% | 0.08 |

| rd_intensity | -0.0002 | -0.05 | 0.05% | 0.13 |

The results are striking. **Analyst revision** (`analyst_rev`) is the only feature with a statistically significant rank IC. Its mean IC of 0.296 is an order of magnitude larger than any other feature, and its t-statistic of 89.6 is extraordinarily high. The long-short portfolio based on analyst revisions would have earned a mean monthly return of 8.66% and an annualized Sharpe ratio of 26.38, which is far above typical equity market Sharpe ratios (usually below 1.0 for well-diversified strategies).

All other features have rank ICs close to zero, with absolute t-statistics below 1.7. Their long-short portfolio returns are economically negligible, and their annualized Sharpe ratios are all below 0.75. The signs of these weak ICs are consistent with some known anomalies (e.g., positive asset growth and accruals ICs in this sample are opposite to the usual negative asset growth and accruals anomalies, but the magnitudes are too small to be meaningful).

## Discussion

The overwhelming dominance of `analyst_rev` raises important questions about the nature of this variable. The data dictionary states that `analyst_rev` is "the change in the mean FY1 EPS forecast during month t+1, scaled by share price. Recorded at the end of month t+1." This definition implies that the variable is measured **concurrently with the return period** (month t+1), not at the end of month t. If the analyst revision is observed during the same month as the return, then the high correlation is not a predictive signal but rather a contemporaneous relationship. Using information that is only available at the end of month t+1 to predict the return over month t+1 constitutes look-ahead bias.

If `analyst_rev` were truly available at the end of month t (i.e., the revision during month t, not t+1), then the strategy would be implementable. However, the data dictionary explicitly states it is recorded at the end of month t+1, making it unusable for predicting `ret_next` without look-ahead bias. Therefore, despite its statistical strength, we cannot treat `analyst_rev` as a valid predictive signal for a real-time trading strategy.

The other nine features show no meaningful predictive power in this sample. This is not entirely surprising: many traditional anomalies have weakened or disappeared in recent decades, especially among large-cap stocks (the index constituents here are likely large-cap given the median log market cap of 7.5, corresponding to about $1.8 billion). The lack of significance for value, profitability, size, investment, and volatility factors suggests that this particular index and time period may not exhibit these cross-sectional patterns.

## Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the `analyst_rev` variable is measured during the return period, making it subject to look-ahead bias; we cannot use it for prediction without a lagged version. Second, the sample is restricted to 500 index constituents per month, which may not represent the broader cross-section of stocks, and the index membership may introduce selection biases. Third, the 120-month sample period is relatively short for evaluating long-term factor performance. Fourth, we only consider univariate signals; multivariate combinations or interactions may reveal predictive power that individual features lack. Finally, transaction costs, liquidity constraints, and short-sale restrictions are not considered in the long-short portfolio calculations.
