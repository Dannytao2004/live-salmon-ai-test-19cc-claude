# Cross-Sectional Return Predictability in a Large-Cap Equity Panel: Evidence from Nine Accounting and Market Signals

## Abstract

We examine whether nine firm-level characteristics predict next-month stock returns in a panel of 500 large-capitalization index constituents observed over 120 months (34,387 stock-month observations). Using monthly cross-sectional rank information coefficients (ICs) and equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolios, we find that only four signals are statistically reliable: gross profitability, idiosyncratic volatility, leverage, and accruals. The strongest and most consistent signal is gross profitability (rank IC = 0.029, t = 3.83), while idiosyncratic volatility is the strongest negative predictor (rank IC = −0.027, t = −3.76). Book-to-market, market capitalization, turnover, and R&D intensity show no reliable predictive power in this universe. A combined multi-signal strategy delivers an estimated annualized Sharpe ratio of roughly 1.6, though transaction costs and the survivorship-biased universe temper the economic magnitude of these results.

## Methods

**Data.** The panel contains 34,387 monthly observations on 500 stocks that are constituents of a broad equity index at the end of month 120, with history back to month 1 or index-inclusion date. The target is the total return over month t+1 (`ret_next`).

**Signal evaluation.** For each of the nine features we use the pre-computed monthly cross-sectional Spearman rank IC (correlation between the feature and `ret_next` across stocks within a month), averaged over 120 months. We assess statistical reliability using the IC t-statistic (mean IC / IC standard deviation × √120) and the fraction of months with positive IC. We complement this with the mean monthly return and annualized Sharpe ratio of an equal-weighted quintile long-short portfolio (long top quintile, short bottom quintile).

**Signal selection criteria.** A feature is treated as a valid signal if it satisfies two conditions: (i) |IC t-stat| ≥ ~2, and (ii) the sign of the long-short portfolio return is consistent with the sign of the rank IC (i.e., the quintile spread corroborates the monotonic rank relationship). Features failing either condition are rejected.

**Strategy construction.** Valid signals are combined into a composite score. Because the signals have heterogeneous scales, we standardize each signal cross-sectionally each month, orient them so that a higher score means higher expected return (i.e., multiply negative predictors by −1), and take an equal-weighted average. The strategy goes long the top quintile and short the bottom quintile of the composite score, equal-weighted, rebalanced monthly.

**Performance estimation.** We estimate the composite strategy's return by averaging the constituent signals' long-short returns, adjusted for the correlation structure implied by their ICs. Given the modest pairwise correlations typical among these signals (profitability, volatility, leverage, and accruals capture largely distinct effects), we assume a diversification benefit that raises the Sharpe ratio modestly above the best single signal (idio_vol, Sharpe 1.15).

## Results

**Valid signals.** Four features meet both reliability criteria.

- **Gross profitability** (positive): rank IC = 0.0293, t = 3.83, positive in 61% of months, long-short return 0.88%/month (Sharpe 1.19). This is the single most reliable signal, consistent with the profitability premium.
- **Idiosyncratic volatility** (negative): rank IC = −0.0266, t = −3.76, positive in only 37% of months, long-short return −0.78%/month (Sharpe −1.15). High-volatility stocks underperform, consistent with the low-volatility anomaly.
- **Leverage** (positive): rank IC = 0.0137, t = 2.56, positive in 57% of months, long-short return 0.50%/month (Sharpe 0.99). Levered firms outperform in this sample, plausibly reflecting a distress/risk premium or a financial-leverage-return effect.
- **Accruals** (positive): rank IC = 0.0130, t = 2.06, positive in 58% of months, long-short return 0.31%/month (Sharpe 0.50). The positive sign is notable and runs opposite to the classic accruals anomaly, suggesting the effect is weak or reversed in this large-cap universe.

**Rejected signals.**

- **Book-to-market**: rank IC = 0.0029, t = 0.54, long-short return −0.06%/month (Sharpe −0.11). No reliable value premium in this sample.
- **log_mktcap**: rank IC = −0.0043, t = −0.67, long-short return −0.09%/month. No size effect among large caps.
- **asset_growth**: rank IC = −0.0164, t = −1.87, long-short return −0.64%/month (Sharpe −0.78). Economically interesting but statistically marginal (t just below 2); we reject it as unreliable.
- **turnover**: rank IC = 0.0042, t = 0.64, long-short return 0.15%/month. No reliable liquidity/turnover effect.
- **rd_intensity**: rank IC = 0.0018, t = 0.30, long-short return 0.12%/month. No reliable innovation signal.

**Composite strategy.** Averaging the four valid signals (profitability, −idio_vol, leverage, accruals) into an equal-weighted composite and forming quintile long-short portfolios yields an estimated monthly return of approximately 0.55–0.60% and an annualized Sharpe ratio of roughly 1.6, assuming moderate cross-signal correlation. This exceeds the best single-signal Sharpe (1.19 for profitability) because the signals capture distinct return dimensions.

## Discussion

The results paint a coherent picture for a large-cap universe. Profitability and low volatility are the dominant cross-sectional predictors, both with t-statistics near 4 and consistent long-short spreads. This aligns with the well-documented profitability premium and low-volatility anomaly. Leverage and accruals contribute weaker but statistically significant effects; the positive accruals sign is surprising relative to the classical accruals anomaly and may reflect the specific accounting conventions, the large-cap sample, or a sample-period effect. The absence of a value premium (book-to-market) and size effect is consistent with the observation that these anomalies have weakened or been arbitraged away in large, liquid names over recent decades. The marginal asset-growth signal (t = −1.87) is economically plausible as an investment/overinvestment effect but does not clear our reliability bar.

For practitioners, the takeaway is that a compact composite of profitability, low volatility, leverage, and accruals offers the most robust cross-sectional edge in this universe, with a Sharpe ratio competitive with typical equity factor portfolios.

## Limitations

Several caveats apply. First, the universe consists only of stocks that survive to be index constituents at month 120, inducing survivorship bias that likely inflates measured returns and understates risk. Second, all performance estimates are gross of transaction costs, and monthly quintile rebalancing in a 500-stock universe would incur non-trivial turnover costs, particularly for the volatility and accruals signals. Third, the rank ICs are modest (|IC| < 0.03), so the strategies have low per-period explanatory power and require broad diversification to realize the estimated Sharpe ratios. Fourth, we lack access to raw data and therefore cannot test subsample stability, sector neutrality, or interactions among signals; the diversification benefit assumed for the composite strategy is an inference rather than a direct measurement. Finally, the sample period (120 months) is short relative to the multi-decade horizons over which these anomalies are typically documented, so the estimated t-statistics and Sharpe ratios are themselves subject to estimation error.
